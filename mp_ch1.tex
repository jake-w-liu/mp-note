\begin{frame}{Where we are going}
\footnotesize
To analyze the special functions of physics we first build the tools of
\emph{complex analysis}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Taylor and Laurent series} represent a function near a point, even
        near a singularity.
  \item \textbf{Analytic functions} (complex differentiable) obey the
        \emph{Cauchy--Riemann equations} and admit power-series expansions.
  \item \textbf{Cauchy's integral theorem} and the \textbf{residue theorem} turn contour
        integrals into a sum of local residues---the tool used later for Gamma/Beta identities
        and real integrals.
  \item A short catalog of \textbf{contour templates} (semicircle, keyhole, Jordan) shows
        how orientation and vanishing-arc estimates convert complex residues into real
        answers.
  \item The \textbf{method of steepest descent} extracts large-parameter asymptotics of
        contour integrals. It gives large-argument Bessel expansions (Ch.~5); its real
        special cases (Laplace method, stationary phase) give Stirling (Ch.~2) and Airy (Ch.~3).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{Taylor series representation}
% ======================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Matching a polynomial to a function}
\footnotesize

A Taylor polynomial replaces a function near one point by a polynomial with the
same value, slope, curvature, and higher derivatives there.

Start at $x=0$ and write the first few terms rather than a general sum:
\[
 p(x)=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3+\cdots+a_Nx^N\qquad(N\ge3).
\]
Differentiate and then set $x=0$:
\begin{align*}
 p(0)&=a_0,\\
 p'(x)&=a_1+2a_2x+3a_3x^2+\cdots,
       &p'(0)&=a_1,\\
 p''(x)&=2a_2+6a_3x+\cdots,
       &p''(0)&=2!a_2,\\
 p^{(3)}(x)&=6a_3+\cdots,
       &p^{(3)}(0)&=3!a_3.
\end{align*}
To match $f$, choose $a_0=f(0)$, $a_1=f'(0)$, $a_2=f''(0)/2!$, and so on.
The pattern is
\begin{equation}
 a_k=\frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}.
 \label{eq:taylor-coef}
\end{equation}
The factorial appears because differentiating $x^k$ exactly $k$ times gives $k!$.


At a different center $x_0$, use powers of the distance $x-x_0$:
\begin{equation}
 T_N(x;x_0)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k.
 \label{eq:taylorx0}
\end{equation}


Adding more terms need not improve the approximation. The Taylor series equals
$f(x)$ when the remainder $R_N(x)=f(x)-T_N(x;x_0)$ tends to zero as
$N\to\infty$:
\begin{equation}
 f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k.
 \label{eq:taylor0}
\end{equation}
Smoothness alone does not give this equality. Take $f(x)=e^{-1/x^2}$ ($x\ne0$), $f(0)=0$.
Each derivative, e.g.\ $f'(x)=(2/x^3)e^{-1/x^2}$, is a polynomial in $1/x$ times
$e^{-1/x^2}$, and with $y=1/x^2$ each term $|x|^{-m}e^{-1/x^2}=y^{m/2}e^{-y}\to0$ as
$x\to0$ (exponentials beat powers). At $0$ itself, $f^{(k)}(0)=\lim_{h\to0}f^{(k-1)}(h)/h$ is a
limit of the same form, so every $f^{(k)}(0)=0$: the Taylor series is
identically $0$, converges everywhere, and still misses $f$ for all $x\ne0$. Such an $f$ is
called \emph{not analytic} at $0$; Section~1.5 shows complex-differentiable functions
never behave this way.


\textbf{Exponential.} Every derivative of $e^x$ equals $e^x$, so every derivative at
zero equals $1$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 e^x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^k}{k!}
 =1+x+\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^3}{3!}+\cdots.
 \label{eq:exp-series}
\end{equation}
The denominator $k!$ grows faster than any power $x^k$, so the terms shrink rapidly
and the series converges for every $x$, where it represents $e^x$.
For example, at $x=0.1$ the first three terms give
$e^{0.1}\approx1+0.1+0.005=1.105$.


\textbf{Cosine and sine.} List the derivative values at zero and divide by the
corresponding factorial:
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrr}
 k&0&1&2&3&4&5\\ \hline
 (\cos)^{(k)}(0)&1&0&-1&0&1&0\\
 (\sin)^{(k)}(0)&0&1&0&-1&0&1
\end{array}
\]
Only even powers survive for cosine; only odd powers survive for sine:
\begin{equation}
 \cos x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k x^{2k}}{(2k)!}
 =1-\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^4}{4!}-\cdots,
 \label{eq:cos-series}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \sin x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}
 =x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}-\cdots.
 \label{eq:sin-series}
\end{equation}
Every derivative of $\cos x$ and $\sin x$ has magnitude at most $1$, so the same
factorial dominance makes both series converge for every $x$.
All three series also converge for every complex argument (the same $k!$ comparison works
with $|z|$ in place of $|x|$). We use them to extend
$e^x$, $\cos x$, and $\sin x$ to complex numbers.
\end{frame}

\section{Complex variables}
% ======================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Imaginary unit, Cartesian and polar form}
\footnotesize

Define the imaginary unit $i$ by $i^2=-1$; the other square root of $-1$ is $-i$.
A complex number has Cartesian form
\[
  z=x+iy,\qquad x=\operatorname{Re}z,\quad y=\operatorname{Im}z,
\]
where $x,y\in\mathbb R$; $\operatorname{Re}z$ and $\operatorname{Im}z$ denote the real
and imaginary parts of $z$.  The powers of $i$ repeat with period four:
\[
  i^{4q}=1,\qquad i^{4q+1}=i,\qquad
  i^{4q+2}=-1,\qquad i^{4q+3}=-i.
\]
Substitute $i\theta$ into the exponential series \eqref{eq:exp-series} and separate even
and odd powers:
\begin{align*}
 e^{i\theta}
  &=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\theta)^n}{n!}\\
  &=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{i^{2k}\theta^{2k}}{(2k)!}
    +\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{i^{2k+1}\theta^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}\\
 \intertext{and since $i^{2k}=(i^2)^k=(-1)^k$ and $i^{2k+1}=i\,i^{2k}=i(-1)^k$,}
  &=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k\theta^{2k}}{(2k)!}
    +i\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k\theta^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}.
\end{align*}
Comparison with \eqref{eq:cos-series}--\eqref{eq:sin-series} gives Euler's formula
\begin{equation}
  e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta.
  \label{eq:euler}
\end{equation}

To split $e^{u+iv}$ (needed to solve $e^w=z$ on the next frame) we need the addition law
$e^{w_1+w_2}=e^{w_1}e^{w_2}$ for complex arguments. By the binomial theorem,
\[
 \frac{(w_1+w_2)^n}{n!}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{w_1^k}{k!}\,\frac{w_2^{\,n-k}}{(n-k)!},
\]
so summing over $n$ collects every product $w_1^k w_2^j/(k!\,j!)$ exactly
once: that is the product of the series for $e^{w_1}$ and $e^{w_2}$.
Taking $w_1=u$, $w_2=iv$ and using \eqref{eq:euler} for $e^{iv}$,
\begin{equation}
 e^{u+iv}=e^u(\cos v+i\sin v),\qquad u,v\in\mathbb R.
 \label{eq:exp-uv}
\end{equation}
The factor $e^u$ determines the modulus, and $v$ determines the angle.

For $z\ne0$, set
\[
  r=|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}>0.
\]
Choose any angle $\theta$ satisfying
$x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$.  Then
\begin{equation}
  z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=re^{i\theta}.
  \label{eq:polar}
\end{equation}
The angle is not unique: if $\theta$ is one argument, then every argument is
$\theta+2\pi k$, $k\in\mathbb Z$.  The principal argument is commonly chosen as
$\operatorname{Arg}z\in(-\pi,\pi]$.
The number $z=0$ has modulus $0$ but no defined argument.

If $z_1=r_1e^{i\theta_1}$ and $z_2=r_2e^{i\theta_2}$, then the phases add by the addition
law above, so
\[
  z_1z_2=r_1r_2e^{i\theta_1}e^{i\theta_2}=r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)},
\]
so moduli multiply and arguments add modulo $2\pi$.


The complex conjugate is
\[
  \overline z=x-iy=re^{-i\theta},
  \qquad
  z\overline z=(x+iy)(x-iy)=x^2+y^2=r^2=|z|^2,
\]
and adding or subtracting $z$ and $\overline z$ isolates the Cartesian components:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Re}z=\frac{z+\overline z}{2},\qquad
  \operatorname{Im}z=\frac{z-\overline z}{2i}.
  \label{eq:re-im-conj}
\end{equation}
Replacing $\theta$ by $-\theta$ in Euler's formula \eqref{eq:euler}, and using $\cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta$, $\sin(-\theta)=-\sin\theta$, gives
$e^{-i\theta}=\cos\theta-i\sin\theta$.  Hence
\begin{align*}
  e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}&=2\cos\theta,\\
  e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}&=2i\sin\theta,
\end{align*}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
  \cos\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2},\qquad
  \sin\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}.
  \label{eq:cos-sin-exp}
\end{equation}
\end{frame}



\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Multivalued functions and branch cuts}
\footnotesize

Powers $z^a$ and $\log z$ appear in many integrals later (e.g.\ the keyhole contour of
Section~1.8, whose answer comes precisely from the jump across a cut), so we must learn
how to make them single-valued.
An inverse relation need not define a single-valued function: $y=x^2$ gives
$x=\pm\sqrt y$ for $y>0$.  A \emph{branch} selects one value continuously on a chosen
domain, as the real square root selects $+\sqrt y$.

\textbf{The complex logarithm.}  Several $w$ have the same $e^w$. Let $z\ne0$ and write
$z=re^{i\theta}$ with $r>0$.  To solve $e^w=z$, write $w=u+iv$ with $u,v\in\mathbb R$.  The
splitting \eqref{eq:exp-uv} gives
\[
 e^w=e^{u+iv}=e^u(\cos v+i\sin v).
\]
Equality with $r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ requires
\[
 e^u=r\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u=\log r,
\]
and
\[
 \cos v=\cos\theta,\quad \sin v=\sin\theta
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v=\theta+2\pi k,
 \qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Thus the complete multivalued logarithm is
\begin{equation}
  \log z=\log|z|+i(\theta+2\pi k),\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
  \label{eq:complex-log-values}
\end{equation}
A principal branch is obtained by choosing one interval of length $2\pi$, commonly
\[
  -\pi<\operatorname{Arg}z<\pi,
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Log}z=\log|z|+i\operatorname{Arg}z.
\]
This analytic branch lives on $\mathbb C\setminus(-\infty,0]$.

A continuous argument increases by $2\pi$ after one counterclockwise turn around
$0$. Thus the logarithm does not return to its starting value: $0$ is a
\emph{branch point}. Removing a \emph{branch cut} prevents such a loop from staying
in our domain. For the principal logarithm, the cut is the nonpositive real axis.
Its two boundary values are
\[
  \operatorname{Log}(-r+i0)=\log r+i\pi,\qquad
  \operatorname{Log}(-r-i0)=\log r-i\pi,
\]
so crossing the cut changes the logarithm by $2\pi i$.

The same mechanism applies to roots.  For example, define $z^{1/2}=e^{\frac12\log z}$ and insert the values \eqref{eq:complex-log-values}:
\[
 z^{1/2}=e^{\frac12\log r}\,e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)/2}
       =\sqrt r\,e^{i\theta/2}e^{i\pi k}
       =(-1)^k\sqrt r\,e^{i\theta/2},
\]
since $e^{i\pi k}=\cos\pi k+i\sin\pi k=(-1)^k$ by \eqref{eq:euler}; so there are two values.  A branch cut permits one of them to be selected continuously.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{The complex derivative and Cauchy--Riemann}
\footnotesize

The complex derivative at $z_0$ is
\begin{equation}
 f'(z_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(z_0+h)-f(z_0)}{h}.
 \label{eq:cderiv}
\end{equation}
The increment $h$ may approach zero from any direction in the complex plane, and
the same limit must result in every case.
\emph{Picture:} $f(z_0+h)\approx f(z_0)+f'(z_0)h$, so near $z_0$ the map $f$ multiplies
every small displacement $h$ by the one complex number $f'(z_0)$: a rotation by
$\arg f'(z_0)$ and a stretch by $|f'(z_0)|$, the same in every direction.
A function is \emph{holomorphic} (analytic) on an open set if this derivative exists
at every point there; it is \emph{entire} if that set is all of $\mathbb C$.

Write $z=x+iy$ and $f(z)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y)$; subscripts denote partial derivatives
($u_x=\partial u/\partial x$, etc.).  Along a real increment $h=\Delta x$, the
difference quotient is
\begin{align*}
 \frac{f(z+h)-f(z)}{h}
 &=\frac{u(x+\Delta x,y)-u(x,y)}{\Delta x}
  +i\,\frac{v(x+\Delta x,y)-v(x,y)}{\Delta x}\\
 &\xrightarrow[\Delta x\to0]{}u_x+iv_x,
\end{align*}
so $f'(z)=u_x+iv_x$.  Along an imaginary increment $h=i\Delta y$ the bookkeeping is the
same, with the extra factor $1/i$ pulled out of the denominator:
\[
 \frac{f(z+i\Delta y)-f(z)}{i\,\Delta y}
 =\frac1i\left[\frac{u(x,y+\Delta y)-u(x,y)}{\Delta y}
   +i\,\frac{v(x,y+\Delta y)-v(x,y)}{\Delta y}\right].
\]
Passing to the limit and using $1/i=-i$,
\[
 f'(z)=\frac{u_y+i v_y}{i}=-i(u_y+i v_y)=v_y-i u_y.
\]
Both directions must give the \emph{same} $f'(z)$; equating $u_x+iv_x=v_y-iu_y$ and
matching real and imaginary parts gives the Cauchy--Riemann equations
\begin{equation}
  u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.
  \label{eq:CR}
\end{equation}
They are necessary for complex differentiability.


\textbf{Working test.} If $u$ and $v$ have continuous first partial derivatives
near a point, the Cauchy--Riemann equations are also sufficient there. Reason: then
$\Delta u\approx u_x\Delta x+u_y\Delta y$ and $\Delta v\approx v_x\Delta x+v_y\Delta y$, and
substituting $u_y=-v_x$, $v_y=u_x$ gives
$\Delta u+i\Delta v\approx(u_x+iv_x)(\Delta x+i\Delta y)$, i.e.\ $\Delta f\approx f'\,h$
with $f'=u_x+iv_x$.
So in practice: find $u,v$, compute four partial derivatives, and compare.

\textbf{Example: $z^2$.}  From $z^2=(x+iy)^2=(x^2-y^2)+i(2xy)$ we get $u_x=2x=v_y$ and
$u_y=-2y=-v_x$, so $z^2$ is entire and $f'(z)=u_x+iv_x=2x+i(2y)=2z$.


\textbf{Counterexample: $\overline z=x-iy$.}  Then $u_x=1\ne-1=v_y$, so CR fails
everywhere.  Equivalently $\overline h/h$ is $1$ for real $h$ and $-1$ for imaginary $h$:
conjugation is a reflection, which no multiplication by a fixed $f'$ can produce.
\end{frame}

\section{Path integrals}
% ======================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Contour integrals and a key circle integral}
\footnotesize

A contour integral is an ordinary integral along a chosen path.
Parameterize the path by $z=\gamma(t)$, $a\le t\le b$, with $\gamma$ piecewise
smooth. Since $dz=\gamma'(t)\,dt$,
\begin{equation}
 \int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=\int_a^b f(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)\,dt.
 \label{eq:pathint}
\end{equation}

Reversing the path reverses the sign: $\int_{-\gamma}f\,dz=-\int_\gamma f\,dz$.
A positively oriented boundary keeps the region on the left: the outer boundary is
counterclockwise, while a boundary around a hole is clockwise.

\textbf{Integer powers on a circle.}  The next computation looks trivial but drives
everything after it: Laurent coefficients and the residue theorem both reduce to it.
Let $n\in\mathbb Z$ and parameterize the positively
oriented circle $|z|=R$ by $z=Re^{i\theta}$, $dz=iRe^{i\theta}\,d\theta$, $0\le\theta\le2\pi$.
Then
\[
 \oint_{|z|=R}z^n\,dz
 =\int_0^{2\pi}R^ne^{in\theta}\bigl(iRe^{i\theta}\bigr)\,d\theta
 =iR^{n+1}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(n+1)\theta}\,d\theta.
\]
If $n=-1$, the exponential is $1$, so
$\oint_{|z|=R}dz/z=i\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta=2\pi i$.
If $n\ne-1$, then
\[
 iR^{n+1}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(n+1)\theta}\,d\theta
 =iR^{n+1}\left[\frac{e^{i(n+1)\theta}}{i(n+1)}\right]_{0}^{2\pi}
 =\frac{R^{n+1}}{n+1}\bigl(e^{2\pi i(n+1)}-1\bigr)=0,
\]
because $n+1$ is an integer.  Translating the circle (substitute $z=z_0+w$, so
$dz=dw$ and $|z-z_0|=R$ becomes $|w|=R$) and writing $\delta_{n,-1}$ for the Kronecker
delta ($1$ if $n=-1$, else $0$), we get
\begin{equation}
 \oint_{|z-z_0|=R}(z-z_0)^n\,dz=2\pi i\,\delta_{n,-1}.
 \label{eq:circ-power}
\end{equation}
\emph{Why only $n=-1$ survives:} for $n\ne-1$, $z^n$ has the single-valued antiderivative
$z^{n+1}/(n+1)$, which returns to its starting value after a closed loop. The antiderivative
of $1/z$ is $\log z$ (differentiate $e^{\log z}=z$: $e^{\log z}(\log z)'=1$), which gains
exactly $2\pi i$ per counterclockwise turn (Section~1.2).
This single fact drives the Laurent coefficients and the residue theorem below.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Cauchy's integral theorem and formula}
\footnotesize

The circle integral showed that $z^n$ with $n\ge0$ integrates to zero around a loop. Cauchy's
theorem says the same for \emph{every} holomorphic function, as long as the loop encloses
no point where $f$ fails to be holomorphic. A connected open region is \emph{simply connected}
if it has no holes (every closed curve in it can shrink to a point inside it).

\begin{block}{Cauchy's integral theorem}
Let $f$ be holomorphic on a simply connected region $D$, and let $\gamma$ be a
closed piecewise smooth contour in $D$.  Then
\[
  \oint_\gamma f(z)\,dz=0.
\]
\end{block}
\textbf{Why zero?} First let $\gamma=\partial E$ be the positively oriented simple
closed boundary of a region $E$. With $f=u+iv$ and $dz=dx+i\,dy$, expand
$f\,dz=(u+iv)(dx+i\,dy)=(u\,dx-v\,dy)+i(v\,dx+u\,dy)$, apply Green's theorem
$\oint_{\partial E}(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=\iint_E(Q_x-P_y)\,dA$ to each real line integral
(first with $P=u$, $Q=-v$, then with $P=v$, $Q=u$), and finally use the
Cauchy--Riemann equations \eqref{eq:CR}:
\begin{align*}
 \oint_\gamma f\,dz
 &=\oint_\gamma(u\,dx-v\,dy)+i\oint_\gamma(v\,dx+u\,dy)\\
 &=\iint_E(-v_x-u_y)\,dA+i\iint_E(u_x-v_y)\,dA\\
 &=\iint_E(u_y-u_y)\,dA+i\iint_E(v_y-v_y)\,dA=0.
\end{align*}
(Green's theorem uses continuous partials; Goursat showed holomorphy alone suffices.)



\begin{block}{Cauchy's integral formula}
Let $\gamma$ be a positively oriented simple closed contour, let $f$ be holomorphic on an
open set containing $\gamma$ and its interior, and let $z_0$ lie inside $\gamma$.  Then
\begin{equation}
  f(z_0)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma\frac{f(z)}{z-z_0}\,dz.
  \label{eq:CIF}
\end{equation}
\end{block}
\framebreak
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.56\linewidth}
\textbf{Why it holds.} Deform $\gamma$ to a small counterclockwise circle
$C_\varepsilon$ about $z_0$. The combined contour $\gamma+s-C_\varepsilon-s$ (right)
encloses only the shaded region, where $f(z)/(z-z_0)$ is holomorphic, so by Cauchy's
theorem its integral is zero. The slit sides $s$ and $-s$ cancel, hence the integrals
over $\gamma$ and $C_\varepsilon$ agree.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.4\linewidth}
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=0.3\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figs/slit-contour.pdf}
\end{column}
\end{columns}

On the small circle $z=z_0+\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, $0\le\theta\le2\pi$, we have
$z-z_0=\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$ and $dz=i\varepsilon e^{i\theta}\,d\theta$, so
$dz/(z-z_0)=i\,d\theta$ and, by \eqref{eq:pathint},
\[
 \oint_{C_\varepsilon}\frac{f(z)}{z-z_0}\,dz
 =i\int_0^{2\pi}f(z_0+\varepsilon e^{i\theta})\,d\theta.
\]
As $\varepsilon\to0$, continuity makes the integrand approach $f(z_0)$, so the
last integral approaches $2\pi i f(z_0)$. This gives \eqref{eq:CIF}.
\emph{Meaning:} the circle integral does not depend on $\varepsilon$, so for every such circle
$f(z_0)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(z_0+\varepsilon e^{i\theta})\,d\theta$: the value at the
centre is the average over the circle---the mean-value property of electrostatic
potentials in charge-free regions.

\end{frame}

\section{Taylor and Laurent series}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=0.99]{Taylor series from contour integration}
\footnotesize

A Taylor series needs every derivative of $f$; Cauchy's formula supplies them.
Differentiate \eqref{eq:CIF} in $z_0$ (contour fixed, away from $z_0$).  Since
$\partial_{z_0}^n(z-z_0)^{-1}=n!/(z-z_0)^{n+1}$, for $n=0,1,2,\ldots$,
\begin{equation}
 f^{(n)}(z_0)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}
  \oint_\gamma\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}}\,dz.
 \label{eq:CIF-deriv}
\end{equation}
In particular every holomorphic function is infinitely differentiable.

\begin{block}{Taylor series theorem}
If $f$ is holomorphic on the disk $|z-z_0|<R$, then for every $|z-z_0|<R$,
\[
 f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(n)}(z_0)}{n!}(z-z_0)^n.
\]
\end{block}
Choose a radius $\rho$ with $|z-z_0|<\rho<R$ and integrate on $C_\rho:|\xi-z_0|=\rho$.  Cauchy's formula \eqref{eq:CIF}, with
$\gamma=C_\rho$, integration variable renamed $\xi$, and interior point now called $z$, gives
\[
 f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{C_\rho}\frac{f(\xi)}{\xi-z}\,d\xi.
\]


The idea is to expand only the kernel, using the geometric series
$1/(1-w)=\sum_{n\ge0}w^n$ with $w=(z-z_0)/(\xi-z_0)$:
\begin{align*}
 \frac1{\xi-z}&=\frac1{(\xi-z_0)-(z-z_0)}
 =\frac1{\xi-z_0}
  \frac1{1-(z-z_0)/(\xi-z_0)}\\
 &=\frac1{\xi-z_0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Bigl(\frac{z-z_0}{\xi-z_0}\Bigr)^n
 =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z-z_0)^n}{(\xi-z_0)^{n+1}}.
\end{align*}
This works because $|(z-z_0)/(\xi-z_0)|=|z-z_0|/\rho<1$ all along the circle,
so we may substitute it and integrate term by term:
\[
 f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(z-z_0)^n
 \underbrace{\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{C_\rho}
     \frac{f(\xi)}{(\xi-z_0)^{n+1}}\,d\xi}_{f^{(n)}(z_0)/n!\text{ by \eqref{eq:CIF-deriv}}}.
\]

\par\framebreak\par

The coefficients are exactly those found by matching derivatives at the start of
the chapter, \eqref{eq:taylor-coef} and \eqref{eq:taylor0}. Here Cauchy's formula also tells us where the series equals $f$.

The key consequence: inside any disk where $f$ is holomorphic, its Taylor series
represents $f$. So the series about $z_0$ converges at least out to the nearest
\emph{singularity} of $f$ (a point where $f$ cannot be made holomorphic;
Section~1.6 classifies them). It converges no farther: a convergent power series may be
differentiated term by term inside its disk, so it is holomorphic there, and a disk
reaching past the singularity would extend $f$ holomorphically across it. Thus being analytic at
$z_0$ and having a Taylor series about $z_0$ are the same property, and the $e^{-1/x^2}$ failure
of Section~1.1 cannot happen: $e^{-1/z^2}$ is not even continuous at $0$ (on $z=iy$ it equals
$e^{1/y^2}$).

\textbf{Why the complex plane matters even for real functions.}
$1/(1+x^2)=1-x^2+x^4-\cdots$ is smooth on all of $\mathbb R$, yet its series about $0$
diverges for $|x|>1$. Nothing on the real line explains this; the reason is the complex
poles $z=\pm i$ of $1/(1+z^2)$, at distance $1$ from $0$.


\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=0.95]{Analytic continuation}

\footnotesize

\textbf{Why we want it.} Chapter~2 defines $\Gamma(z)$ by an integral that converges only
for $\operatorname{Re}z>0$, then extends $\Gamma$ to the left half-plane. Such an extension is
an analytic continuation. The simplest case: a Taylor series is local and may stop
converging although the function stays holomorphic farther away.

\textbf{Example.} $f(z)=1/(1-z)=1+z+z^2+\cdots$ converges only for $|z|<1$, because
the nearest singularity is $z=1$; yet $1/(1-z)$ is holomorphic at every other point.
To go beyond this disk, re-expand about a new center where $f$ is already known, say
$z=-\tfrac12$:
\[
    f(z)
    =\frac{1}{\frac32-(z+\frac12)}
    =\frac23\,
      \frac{1}{1-\frac23(z+\frac12)}
    =\frac23\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
      \left[\frac23\left(z+\frac12\right)\right]^n,
\]
the geometric series $\sum w^n=1/(1-w)$ with $w=\frac23(z+\frac12)$. It converges exactly
when $|w|<1$, that is, for $|z+\frac12|<\frac32$: a new disk that overlaps the first and
reaches farther (to $z=-2$). Call the first disk $D_0$ and the new one $D_1$; repeating gives
$D_0\to D_1\to D_2\to\cdots$ (figure on the next slide), which carries $f$ step by step
through a larger connected region.

\framebreak

\textbf{Why the result is unique.} It is forced by the following theorem.

\begin{block}{Identity theorem}
Let $f,g$ be holomorphic on a connected open set $\Omega$. If they agree on a set with
a limit point inside $\Omega$ --- a sequence $z_n\to a$, a segment, or a small
disk --- then $f=g$ on all of $\Omega$.
\end{block}
Equivalently (set $g=0$): \emph{zeros of a nonzero holomorphic function are isolated} ---
on a connected domain, a function that vanishes on a whole segment, or on a sequence
$z_n\to a$, already vanishes on all of $\Omega$.

\emph{Proof} (the figure beside Step~2 shows both steps). Set $h=f-g$.

\textbf{Step 1: a limit point of zeros makes a zero disk.} Suppose $h(z_n)=0$ on a
sequence $z_n\to a$, $z_n\ne a$. If the Taylor series of $h$ at $a$ had a first nonzero
term $c_k(z-a)^k$, then $h(z)=(z-a)^k[c_k+c_{k+1}(z-a)+\cdots]$ would be nonzero at
every $z\ne a$ near $a$, contradicting $h(z_n)=0$. So every coefficient vanishes and
$h\equiv0$ on a disk $D_0$ about $a$.

\framebreak

\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.52\textwidth}
\textbf{Step 2: overlaps propagate.} $\Omega$ is connected, so any $b\in\Omega$ is
joined to $a$ by a path; cover it by finitely many overlapping disks
$D_0,D_1,\dots,D_N\subset\Omega$. Each overlap is an open set on which $h=0$ already,
so Step~1 applied inside the next disk gives $h\equiv0$ there; after $D_N$, $h(b)=0$.
Since $b$ was arbitrary, $h\equiv0$ on $\Omega$.
\column{0.44\textwidth}
\begin{center}
    \safeincludegraphics[width=\linewidth]
    {figs/identity-theorem-disks.pdf}
\end{center}
\end{columns}

\framebreak

\textbf{How to apply it.} Two patterns recur in later chapters.
\textbf{(a) Uniqueness of continuation.} Two continuations of the same function agree
on the original domain, which contains limit points; the theorem forces them to agree
wherever both are defined: however $\Gamma$ is continued, the result is the same
function.
\textbf{(b) Proving identities.} Two functions that agree on a segment agree on their
whole connected domain. Chapter~2 uses this for $\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\pi/\sin(\pi z)$:
an integral proves it for real $a\in(0,1)$, then the theorem extends it to every
$z\notin\mathbb Z$ --- no further computation needed.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=0.99]{Laurent series}
\footnotesize

A Taylor series uses nonnegative powers. Near a singularity we also need
$1/(z-z_0),1/(z-z_0)^2,\ldots$. Their combination is a \emph{Laurent series}.

Suppose $f$ is holomorphic in the annulus $r<|z-z_0|<R$. For $z$ in it, take
counterclockwise circles $C_1,C_2$ about $z_0$ of radii $r<\rho_2<|z-z_0|<\rho_1<R$.
Cauchy's formula \eqref{eq:CIF} for the ring between them (slit as in its proof) gives,
with a minus sign because the ring's inner boundary runs clockwise,
\[
 f(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{C_1}\frac{f(\xi)\,d\xi}{\xi-z}-\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{C_2}\frac{f(\xi)\,d\xi}{\xi-z}.
\]
On $C_1$, expand the kernel exactly as on the Taylor slide: nonnegative powers of $z-z_0$.
On $C_2$ now $|\xi-z_0|<|z-z_0|$, so expand the other way:
\[
 \frac1{\xi-z}=\frac{-1}{(z-z_0)\bigl[1-\frac{\xi-z_0}{z-z_0}\bigr]}
 =-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\xi-z_0)^n}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}},
\]
which produces the negative powers. Hence $f$ has a Laurent expansion, convergent
throughout the annulus:
\begin{equation}
 f(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_n(z-z_0)^n
 =\underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(z-z_0)^n}_{\text{regular part}}
  +\underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{-n}(z-z_0)^{-n}}_{\text{principal part}}.
 \label{eq:laurent}
\end{equation}

\par\framebreak\par

To extract a coefficient, multiply by
$(z-z_0)^{-k-1}$ and integrate on a centered circle
$\gamma:|z-z_0|=\rho$, where $r<\rho<R$, oriented counterclockwise:
\[
 \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma
 \frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{k+1}}\,dz
 =\frac{1}{2\pi i}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_n
   \oint_\gamma(z-z_0)^{n-k-1}\,dz
 =\frac{1}{2\pi i}\,a_k\,(2\pi i),
\]
because by \eqref{eq:circ-power} only the exponent $n-k-1=-1$, i.e.\ $n=k$, gives a
nonzero integral. Therefore, for every integer $k$,
\begin{equation}
 a_k=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma
      \frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{k+1}}\,dz.
 \label{eq:laurent-coef}
\end{equation}
If the principal part is empty, the series extends holomorphically to the full disk
and is a Taylor series.


\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Classifying singularities}
\footnotesize

The residue formulas of Section~1.7 depend on how badly $f$ misbehaves at a point, so we
sort singular points by their Laurent series.
A point $z_0$ is a \emph{singular point} of $f$ when $f$ fails to be holomorphic there.
It is an \emph{isolated singularity} if $f$ is holomorphic on a punctured disk
$0<|z-z_0|<R$. That is the annulus of \eqref{eq:laurent} with $r=0$, so there
$f(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_n(z-z_0)^n$. Then:
\begin{itemize}\footnotesize
 \item The singularity is \textbf{removable} if $a_n=0$ for every $n<0$.  Defining
       $f(z_0)=a_0$ makes $f$ holomorphic at $z_0$.  Example:
       $\sin z/z$ at $0$: dividing \eqref{eq:sin-series} (with $x$ replaced by $z$) by $z$ gives
       $1-z^2/3!+z^4/5!-\cdots$, with no negative powers, so $a_0=1$.
 \item It is a \textbf{pole of order $m$}, where $m\ge1$ is an integer, if $a_{-m}\ne0$
       and $a_n=0$ for every $n<-m$.         Equivalently,
       \[
       f(z)=\frac{h(z)}{(z-z_0)^m},\qquad h\text{ holomorphic},\quad h(z_0)\ne0.
       \]
       Multiplying by $(z-z_0)^m$ removes exactly the highest negative power.
 \item It is \textbf{essential} if infinitely many negative-power coefficients are
       nonzero.  For example, substituting $x\to1/z$, $z\ne0$, in the sine series
       \eqref{eq:sin-series}, which converges for every complex argument, gives
       \[
        \sin\left(\frac1z\right)
        =\frac1z-\frac1{3!z^3}+\frac1{5!z^5}-\cdots,
       \]
       so $\sin(1/z)$ has an essential singularity at $0$.
\end{itemize}
A function holomorphic except for isolated poles is \emph{meromorphic}.  For example,
$1/\sin z$ is meromorphic, with simple poles exactly at $z=n\pi$, $n\in\mathbb Z$.
\emph{Why only there:} the even/odd splitting that gave \eqref{eq:euler} works for complex
$z$ too, so \eqref{eq:cos-sin-exp} holds for complex arguments and $\sin z=(e^{iz}-e^{-iz})/(2i)$, which vanishes iff $e^{2iz}=1$. For $z=x+iy$,
\eqref{eq:exp-uv} gives $e^{2iz}=e^{-2y}(\cos2x+i\sin2x)$; modulus $1$ forces $y=0$, and
then $\cos2x=1$ forces $x=n\pi$. The poles are simple because
$\sin z\approx\cos(n\pi)(z-n\pi)$ with $\cos n\pi=\pm1\ne0$.

A branch point is not an isolated singularity: continuing $\log z$ once around $0$ adds
$2\pi i$, so no single-valued branch is holomorphic on a whole punctured disk about $0$.

Behaviour for large $|z|$ (which decides whether a big arc vanishes, Section~1.8) is
classified the same way through $F(w)=f(1/w)$ at $w=0$; e.g.\ a degree-$m$ polynomial has a
pole of order $m$ at $\infty$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=0.95]{The residue theorem}
\footnotesize

\textbf{Why we want it.} Many real integrals of physics can be closed into a contour
integral (Section~1.8). The residue theorem evaluates such an integral from a single
Laurent coefficient at each enclosed singularity.

For an isolated singularity $z_0$, the \emph{residue} is the Laurent coefficient
$\operatorname{Res}(f,z_0)=a_{-1}$ of the punctured-disk expansion (Section~1.6).
On a small counterclockwise circle $C$ about $z_0$ enclosing no other singularity,
integrate that expansion term by term. By \eqref{eq:circ-power} every power except
$(z-z_0)^{-1}$ integrates to zero, so
\begin{equation}
 \oint_C f(z)\,dz
 =\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_n\oint_C(z-z_0)^n\,dz
 =2\pi i\,a_{-1}
 =2\pi i\,\operatorname{Res}(f,z_0).
 \label{eq:res-single}
\end{equation}


\begin{block}{Residue theorem}
Let $f$ be holomorphic inside and on a positively oriented simple closed contour $\gamma$,
except for finitely many isolated singularities $z_1,\ldots,z_N$ in the interior.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \oint_\gamma f(z)\,dz
 =2\pi i\sum_{k=1}^{N}\operatorname{Res}(f,z_k).
 \label{eq:res-thm}
\end{equation}
\end{block}
\textbf{Why.} By Cauchy's theorem, with slits as in the proof of \eqref{eq:CIF}, shrink
$\gamma$ onto small counterclockwise circles, one around each $z_k$. Each circle contributes
$2\pi i\operatorname{Res}(f,z_k)$ by \eqref{eq:res-single}.


\textbf{Residue at a pole of order $m$.}  Write $f=h/(z-z_0)^m$ with $h(z)=(z-z_0)^mf(z)$
holomorphic, $h(z_0)\ne0$ (Section~1.6). Taylor-expanding $h$ and dividing,
\[
 f(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{h^{(j)}(z_0)}{j!}(z-z_0)^{j-m}.
\]
The exponent equals $-1$ exactly when $j=m-1$.  Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Res}(f,z_0)
 =\frac{1}{(m-1)!}
  \left.\frac{d^{m-1}}{dz^{m-1}}
  \left[(z-z_0)^m f(z)\right]\right|_{z=z_0}.
 \label{eq:res-order-m}
\end{equation}
For $m=1$ this reduces to $\operatorname{Res}(f,z_0)=\lim_{z\to z_0}(z-z_0)f(z)$; for $m=2$
it is the first derivative of $(z-z_0)^2f(z)$ at $z_0$.

\textbf{A quotient at a simple zero.} Let $p,q$ be holomorphic near $z_0$ with $q(z_0)=0$,
$q'(z_0)\ne0$. Then $q(z)=q'(z_0)(z-z_0)+\cdots$, so cancel $z-z_0$ before taking the limit:
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Res}\left(\frac pq,z_0\right)
 =\lim_{z\to z_0}\frac{p(z)}{q(z)/(z-z_0)}
 =\frac{p(z_0)}{q'(z_0)}.
 \label{eq:res-ratio}
\end{equation}
When $p(z_0)=0$, the singularity is removable and the formula gives zero.

\textbf{Examples.} By \eqref{eq:res-ratio} with $p=1$, $q=z^2+1$, $q'(z)=2z$:
$\operatorname{Res}\bigl(1/(z^2+1),i\bigr)=1/q'(i)=1/(2i)$. At the double pole of
$1/(z^2+1)^2=1/[(z-i)^2(z+i)^2]$, the order-$2$ formula \eqref{eq:res-order-m} with
$(z-i)^2/(z^2+1)^2=1/(z+i)^2$ gives
\[
 \operatorname{Res}\left(\frac1{(z^2+1)^2},i\right)
 =\left.\frac{d}{dz}\frac1{(z+i)^2}\right|_{z=i}
  =\left.\frac{-2}{(z+i)^3}\right|_{z=i}
 =-\frac{2}{(2i)^3}
  =-\frac{2}{-8i}
  =\frac1{4i}.
\]

\textbf{Quick checks on $|z|=1$.} Dividing the sine series \eqref{eq:sin-series} by $z^2$,
$\sin z/z^2=1/z-z/3!+\cdots$, so $\operatorname{Res}(\sin z/z^2,0)=1$ and, by
\eqref{eq:res-single}, $\oint_{|z|=1}(\sin z)/z^2\,dz=2\pi i$.  By contrast,
$z^2/\sin z=z/(1-z^2/3!+\cdots)$ extends holomorphically through $0$ (value $0$), and the
other zeros $n\pi$ of $\sin z$ (Section~1.6) lie outside the unit circle, so Cauchy's
integral theorem gives $\oint_{|z|=1}z^2/\sin z\,dz=0$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Real integrals by a semicircular contour}
\footnotesize

\textbf{Why go complex.} A real antiderivative of $1/(1+x^4)$ is messy, and
$\cos x/(1+x^2)$ has no elementary one at all. Closing the real line into a loop turns
such an integral into a sum of a few residues.

Let $\Gamma_R$ consist of the real segment from $-R$ to $R$ and the upper semicircle
$C_R:z=Re^{i\theta}$, $0\le\theta\le\pi$.  For an integrand with no pole on the real
segment,
\begin{equation}
 \oint_{\Gamma_R}f(z)\,dz
 =\int_{-R}^{R}f(x)\,dx+\int_{C_R}f(z)\,dz.
 \label{eq:semicircle-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The magnitude of an integral is at most the maximum integrand magnitude $M$
times the path length $L$ (the $ML$ estimate). With $M_R=\max_{z\in C_R}|f(z)|$ and arc
length $\pi R$,
\[
 \left|\int_{C_R}f(z)\,dz\right|\le\pi R M_R .
\]
So the arc vanishes whenever $RM_R\to0$. Then the residue theorem \eqref{eq:res-thm}
applied to \eqref{eq:semicircle-decomposition}, with $R\to\infty$, gives (for a convergent
real integral)
\begin{equation}
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx
 =2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}z_k>0}\operatorname{Res}(f,z_k),
 \label{eq:semicircle-result}
\end{equation}
summed over the poles $z_k$ of $f$ in the upper half-plane.
For an integrand containing $e^{iaz}$ with $a>0$, close \emph{upward}: there
$|e^{iaz}|=e^{-ay}\le1$, while below the axis the factor grows like $e^{a|y|}$.

\begin{center}
  \safeincludegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figs/semicircle-contour.pdf}
\end{center}


\textbf{Example: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cos x/(1+x^2)\,dx$.}  Consider
$J=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{ix}/(1+x^2)\,dx$, whose real part is the wanted integral.
For $z=x+iy$ in the upper half-plane, $|e^{iz}|=|e^{ix-y}|=e^{-y}\le1$.
The denominator needs a \emph{lower} bound: the reverse triangle inequality
$|a+b|\ge\bigl||a|-|b|\bigr|$ (from $|a|=|(a+b)-b|\le|a+b|+|b|$, and the same with $a,b$
swapped) with $a=z^2$, $b=1$ gives $|1+z^2|\ge R^2-1$ on $|z|=R>1$, so
\[
 \left|\int_{C_R}\frac{e^{iz}}{1+z^2}\,dz\right|
 \le\frac{\pi R}{R^2-1}\longrightarrow0.
\]

Only the pole $z=i$ lies in the upper half-plane.  As for $1/(z^2+1)$ in the residue
examples, \eqref{eq:res-ratio} with $q=1+z^2$, $q'(i)=2i$ applies, now with
$p(i)=e^{i\cdot i}=e^{-1}$:
\[
 \operatorname{Res}\left(\frac{e^{iz}}{1+z^2},i\right)=\frac{e^{-1}}{2i}.
\]
Hence, by \eqref{eq:semicircle-result},
\[
 J=2\pi i\frac{e^{-1}}{2i}=\frac{\pi}{e}.
\]
Taking real parts gives
\begin{equation}
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{e}.
 \label{eq:cos-rational-integral}
\end{equation}
The imaginary part vanishes consistently because $\sin x/(1+x^2)$ is odd.

\framebreak

\textbf{Example: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx/(1+x^4)$.}
Close in the upper half-plane.  The same inequality gives $|1+z^4|\ge R^4-1$, so by $ML$
$|\int_{C_R}|\le \pi R/(R^4-1)\to0$.  The poles are the fourth roots of $-1$:
\[
 z_k=e^{i(\pi+2k\pi)/4},\qquad k=0,1,2,3.
\]
Those in the upper half-plane are the simple poles
\[
 z_0=e^{i\pi/4},\qquad z_1=e^{i3\pi/4}.
\]
Formula \eqref{eq:res-ratio} with $p=1$, $q=1+z^4$, $q'=4z^3$ gives each residue as
\[
 \operatorname{Res}\Bigl(\frac1{1+z^4},z_j\Bigr)
  =\frac1{4z_j^3}=\frac{z_j}{4z_j^4}=-\frac{z_j}{4},
\]
where the last step uses $z_j^4=-1$.
Adding the two upper-half-plane residues and using Euler's formula \eqref{eq:euler},
$e^{i\pi/4}+e^{3i\pi/4}=\frac{1+i}{\sqrt2}+\frac{-1+i}{\sqrt2}=i\sqrt2$,
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^1\operatorname{Res}\left(\frac1{1+z^4},z_j\right)
 =-\frac{z_0+z_1}{4}
 =-\frac{i\sqrt2}{4}=-\frac{i}{2\sqrt2}.
\]
The semicircle formula \eqref{eq:semicircle-result} therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{1+x^4}
  =2\pi i\Bigl(-\frac{i}{2\sqrt2}\Bigr)
  =\frac{\pi}{\sqrt2}.
 \label{eq:one-plus-x4}
\end{equation}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Keyhole contour: $\int_0^\infty x^a/(1+x)^2\,dx$}
\footnotesize

Let
\[
 I(a)=\int_0^\infty\frac{x^a}{(1+x)^2}\,dx,
 \qquad -1<a<1.
\]
The endpoint conditions follow directly: near $0$ the integrand behaves as $x^a$, which
is integrable when $a>-1$; near infinity it behaves as $x^{a-2}$, which is integrable
when $a<1$.

\textbf{Idea.} Put the branch cut of $z^a$ on the integration axis, and run a contour out
along one side of the cut and back along the other. For a single-valued integrand the two
sides would cancel. Here $z^a$ jumps by the factor $e^{2\pi ia}$ across the cut, so the two
sides leave $(1-e^{2\pi ia})I(a)$, which the residue theorem evaluates.

For $a\ne0$ choose the branch whose cut runs along the \emph{positive} real axis,
\[
 z^a=e^{a\ell(z)},\qquad
 \ell(z)=\log|z|+i\arg z,\qquad 0<\arg z<2\pi ,
\]
rather than the principal logarithm used earlier.
Use a counterclockwise keyhole with outer radius $R$ and inner radius $\varepsilon$.
The hole keeps the branch point $0$ outside, since a branch point has no residue.

\begin{center}
  \safeincludegraphics[height=0.5\textheight]{figs/keyhole-contour.pdf}
\end{center}

On the upper side, $z=x$ and the contribution is
\[
 I_{\varepsilon,R}(a)=\int_\varepsilon^R\frac{x^a}{(1+x)^2}\,dx.
\]

On the lower side, $z=xe^{2\pi i}$ (approached as $\arg z\to2\pi$ from below), so $\ell(z)=\log x+2\pi i$ and
$z^a=e^{a\ell(z)}=e^{a\log x}e^{2\pi ia}=x^ae^{2\pi ia}$.  The orientation is from
$R$ back to $\varepsilon$, so the contribution is
\[
 \int_R^\varepsilon\frac{x^ae^{2\pi ia}}{(1+x)^2}\,dx
 =-e^{2\pi ia}I_{\varepsilon,R}(a).
\]
Thus the two straight segments sum to
\[
 (1-e^{2\pi ia})I_{\varepsilon,R}(a).
\]


Both arcs die by the $ML$ estimate. On $|z|=R>1$,
$|z^a|=e^{a\log|z|}=R^a$, the reverse triangle inequality gives $|1+z|\ge R-1$, and the arc
length is $2\pi R$. Hence
\[
 \left|\int_{C_R}\frac{z^a}{(1+z)^2}\,dz\right|
 \le \frac{2\pi R^{a+1}}{(R-1)^2}\longrightarrow0
 \qquad(\text{it behaves like }2\pi R^{a-1},\ a<1).
\]
On $|z|=\varepsilon<1$,
$|z^a|=\varepsilon^a$, $|1+z|\ge1-\varepsilon$, and the arc length is
$2\pi\varepsilon$, so
\[
 \left|\int_{C_\varepsilon}\frac{z^a}{(1+z)^2}\,dz\right|
 \le \frac{2\pi\varepsilon^{a+1}}{(1-\varepsilon)^2}\longrightarrow0
 \qquad(a>-1).
\]

The only enclosed singularity is the double pole at $z=-1$.  Since
$(z+1)^2\cdot z^a/(1+z)^2=z^a$, formula \eqref{eq:res-order-m} with $m=2$ gives the
derivative of $z^a$ at $-1$. On this branch $(z^a)'=az^{a-1}$, with
$z^{a-1}=e^{(a-1)\ell(z)}$ (chain rule; $\ell'(z)=1/z$ as in Section~1.4). Since $\ell(-1)=i\pi$ and
$e^{-i\pi}=-1$,
\[
 \operatorname{Res}\left(\frac{z^a}{(1+z)^2},-1\right)
 =a e^{i\pi(a-1)}=a e^{i\pi a}e^{-i\pi}=-a e^{i\pi a}.
\]
Now apply the residue theorem \eqref{eq:res-thm} to the keyhole and let $R\to\infty$,
$\varepsilon\to0$. The arcs vanish, and the segments give $(1-e^{2\pi ia})I(a)$:
\[
 (1-e^{2\pi ia})I(a)=2\pi i\,(-a e^{i\pi a})=-2\pi i a e^{i\pi a}.
\]


Factor the denominator (pull out $e^{i\pi a}$, then use
$e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}=2i\sin\theta$ from \eqref{eq:cos-sin-exp}):
\begin{align*}
 1-e^{2\pi ia}
 &=e^{i\pi a}(e^{-i\pi a}-e^{i\pi a})\\
 &=-2i e^{i\pi a}\sin(\pi a).
\end{align*}
Therefore
\begin{align*}
 I(a)
 &=\frac{-2\pi i a e^{i\pi a}}
        {-2i e^{i\pi a}\sin(\pi a)}\\
 &=\frac{\pi a}{\sin(\pi a)}.
\end{align*}
Thus
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^\infty\frac{x^a}{(1+x)^2}\,dx
 =\frac{\pi a}{\sin(\pi a)},\qquad -1<a<1.
 \label{eq:keyhole-xa}
\end{equation}
At $a=0$, the right-hand side has limit $1=\int_0^\infty dx/(1+x)^2$.

\medskip
\textbf{Companion integral for Chapter~2.} The reflection formula
$\Gamma(a)\Gamma(1-a)=\pi/\sin(\pi a)$ of Chapter~2 rests on
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^\infty\frac{x^{-a}}{1+x}\,dx
 =\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi a)},\qquad 0<a<1.
 \label{eq:keyhole2}
\end{equation}
Use the same keyhole with $z^{-a}/(1+z)$. Now the lower side carries the factor
$e^{-2\pi ia}$, and the simple pole at $-1$ has residue equal to the numerator there:
$(-1)^{-a}=e^{-a\ell(-1)}=e^{-i\pi a}$. By $ML$ the arcs are at most
$2\pi R^{1-a}/(R-1)$ and $2\pi\varepsilon^{1-a}/(1-\varepsilon)$, which vanish because
$0<a<1$. Therefore
\[
 (1-e^{-2\pi ia})\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{-a}}{1+x}\,dx
 =2\pi i e^{-i\pi a}.
\]
Since $1-e^{-2\pi ia}=e^{-i\pi a}(e^{i\pi a}-e^{-i\pi a})=2i e^{-i\pi a}\sin(\pi a)$,
dividing gives \eqref{eq:keyhole2}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Jordan's lemma and $\int \sin x/x\,dx$}
\footnotesize

\textbf{Goal.} Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sin x/x\,dx$. Since
$\sin x=\operatorname{Im}e^{ix}$, integrate $e^{iz}/z$ around the upper semicircle. Two
obstacles appear, and each needs a tool:
\begin{itemize}
 \item the pole $z=0$ lies \emph{on} the path, so we detour around it on a small
       semicircle (Tool~1);
 \item on the big arc the plain $ML$ bound gives only $\pi R\cdot(1/R)=\pi$, which does not
       vanish, so we need a sharper estimate (Tool~2, Jordan's lemma).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Tool 1: a small clockwise semicircle around a real-axis pole.}
Near a simple pole $z_0$ the Laurent expansion \eqref{eq:laurent} has one principal term:
$f(z)=c/(z-z_0)+g(z)$ with $c=a_{-1}=\operatorname{Res}(f,z_0)$ and $g$ (the regular part)
holomorphic, hence bounded, near $z_0$. Parameterize the upper indentation by
$z=z_0+\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, traversed from $\theta=\pi$ to $0$. Then
$z-z_0=\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$ and $dz=i\varepsilon e^{i\theta}\,d\theta$, so
\[
 \frac{c}{z-z_0}\,dz
 =\frac{c}{\varepsilon e^{i\theta}}\,i\varepsilon e^{i\theta}\,d\theta
 =ic\,d\theta,
 \qquad ic\int_\pi^0d\theta=ic(0-\pi)=-i\pi c.
\]
The bounded part contributes at most its bound times $\pi\varepsilon\to0$, so, with the
minus sign coming from the clockwise orientation,
\begin{equation}
 \int_{C_\varepsilon}f(z)\,dz\longrightarrow
 -i\pi\operatorname{Res}(f,z_0).\label{eq:indent}
\end{equation}

\framebreak

\textbf{Tool 2: Jordan's lemma.} For $a>0$ and $z=Re^{i\theta}$, $0\le\theta\le\pi$,
$|e^{iaz}|=|e^{iaR\cos\theta}e^{-aR\sin\theta}|=e^{-aR\sin\theta}$ is tiny except near
$\theta=0,\pi$, where it does not decay. But there the arc is short: $aR\sin\theta\lesssim1$
only for $\theta\lesssim1/(aR)$, a length of about $1/a$ at each end however large $R$ is,
so the arc integral is only of order $M_R/a$.

\textbf{Jordan's lemma.} \emph{Let $a>0$, let $C_R$ be the upper semicircle, and let
$M_R=\max_{z\in C_R}|f(z)|$. If $M_R\to0$ as $R\to\infty$, then}
\[
 \int_{C_R}f(z)e^{iaz}\,dz\longrightarrow0.
\]
\textbf{Proof.} With $|dz|=R\,d\theta$ and the bound above: since
$\sin(\pi-\theta)=\sin\theta$, the two halves of $[0,\pi]$ contribute equally, and on
$[0,\frac\pi2]$ concavity gives $\sin\theta\ge2\theta/\pi$ (the graph lies above its chord
from $(0,0)$ to $(\frac\pi2,1)$). Hence
\begin{align*}
 \left|\int_{C_R}f(z)e^{iaz}\,dz\right|
 &\le M_R R\int_0^\pi e^{-aR\sin\theta}\,d\theta
 =2M_R R\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{-aR\sin\theta}\,d\theta\\
 &\le2M_R R\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{-2aR\theta/\pi}\,d\theta
 =2M_R R\left[-\frac{\pi}{2aR}e^{-2aR\theta/\pi}\right]_0^{\pi/2}\\
 &=2M_R R\cdot\frac{\pi}{2aR}\bigl(1-e^{-aR}\bigr)
 \le\frac{\pi M_R}{a}\longrightarrow0.
\end{align*}

\textbf{Example: the Dirichlet integral.} Close $e^{iz}/z$ with $C_R$ and a clockwise small
semicircle $C_\varepsilon$ above $0$; this contour encloses no pole, so by Cauchy's theorem
\[
 \int_{-R}^{-\varepsilon}\frac{e^{ix}}x\,dx
 +\int_{C_\varepsilon}\frac{e^{iz}}z\,dz
 +\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\frac{e^{ix}}x\,dx
 +\int_{C_R}\frac{e^{iz}}z\,dz=0 .
\]
\begin{itemize}
 \item \emph{Large arc:} $f(z)=1/z$ has $M_R=1/R\to0$, so by Jordan's lemma it tends to $0$.
 \item \emph{Small arc:} $\operatorname{Res}(e^{iz}/z,0)=\lim_{z\to0}z\cdot e^{iz}/z=1$, so by
       \eqref{eq:indent} it tends to $-i\pi$.
 \item \emph{Real pieces:} they must be combined symmetrically, because $\cos x/x$ behaves
       as $1/x$ near zero. This symmetric limit, $\int_{-R}^{-\varepsilon}g\,dx+
       \int_\varepsilon^R g\,dx$ with $\varepsilon\to0^+$, $R\to\infty$, is the
       \emph{Cauchy principal value} $\operatorname{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g\,dx$.
\end{itemize}
Letting $R\to\infty$, $\varepsilon\to0$ gives
$0=\operatorname{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{ix}/x\,dx-i\pi$, so
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ix}}x\,dx=i\pi.
 \label{eq:pv-exponential-over-x}
\end{equation}
Taking imaginary parts (no principal value needed, as $\sin x/x\to1$ at $0$),
\begin{equation}
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx=\pi.
 \label{eq:dirichlet-integral}
\end{equation}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Sampling kernels: writing a sum as an integral}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Why.} Residue calculus also runs backwards: a \emph{sum} $\sum f(n)$ can be written
as a contour integral, which is then evaluated from the few residues of $f$ itself. This
gives closed forms for sums such as $\sum 1/(n^2+a^2)$.

\textbf{Integer samples.} The kernel
\[
 \pi\cot\pi z=\frac{\pi\cos\pi z}{\sin\pi z}
\]
has a simple pole at every integer $n$ (the zeros of $\sin\pi z$), each with residue $1$:
by \eqref{eq:res-ratio} with $p=\pi\cos\pi z$, $q=\sin\pi z$, $q'(n)=\pi\cos n\pi\ne0$,
the residue is $\pi\cos n\pi/(\pi\cos n\pi)=1$. Think of it as an
\emph{integer-sampling kernel}: multiplying by $f$ weights the pole at $n$ by $f(n)$, so
$\operatorname{Res}(f\,\pi\cot\pi z,n)=f(n)$. Hence, if $C$ encloses the integers
$n_1,\ldots,n_2$ and $f$ has no poles inside or on $C$, the residue theorem
\eqref{eq:res-thm} gives
\[
 \sum_{m=n_1}^{n_2}f(m)
 =\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C f(z)\,\pi\cot\pi z\,dz .
\]
If $f$ does have poles inside $C$, their residues of $f\,\pi\cot\pi z$ are added on the left.

\framebreak

\textbf{Example: $\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}1/(n^2+a^2)$, $a>0$.} Take
$f(z)=1/(z^2+a^2)$ and let $C_N$ be the square with corners $(N+\tfrac12)(\pm1\pm i)$,
which passes midway between integers.
\begin{itemize}
 \item \emph{The integral vanishes as $N\to\infty$.} $\cot\pi z$ stays bounded on $C_N$, say
       $|\cot\pi z|\le2$: the square passes midway between its poles, and far from the real
       axis $\cot\pi z\to\mp i$ (as $y\to\pm\infty$). Since $|z|\ge N+\tfrac12$ there, the reverse
       triangle inequality gives $|z^2+a^2|\ge(N+\tfrac12)^2-a^2$. The length is
       $8(N+\tfrac12)$, so by $ML$
       \[
        \left|\oint_{C_N}\frac{\pi\cot\pi z}{z^2+a^2}\,dz\right|
        \le\frac{8(N+\tfrac12)\cdot2\pi}{(N+\tfrac12)^2-a^2}\longrightarrow0 .
       \]
 \item \emph{Residues of $f$.} At $z_0=\pm ia$, \eqref{eq:res-ratio} with $q=z^2+a^2$,
       $q'=2z$, gives $\pi\cot(\pi z_0)/(2z_0)$. By \eqref{eq:cos-sin-exp} at complex
       argument (Section~1.6), with $\cosh y=(e^y+e^{-y})/2$, $\sinh y=(e^y-e^{-y})/2$,
       $\coth=\cosh/\sinh$:
       \[ \cos(iy)=\frac{e^{-y}+e^{y}}2=\cosh y,\qquad \sin(iy)=\frac{e^{-y}-e^{y}}{2i}=i\sinh y, \]
       $\cot(iy)=\cosh y/(i\sinh y)=-i\coth y$, and $\cot$ is odd, so both residues equal
       $\dfrac{\mp i\pi\coth\pi a}{\pm2ia}=-\dfrac{\pi\coth\pi a}{2a}$.
\end{itemize}
The residue theorem on $C_N$, with $N\to\infty$, gives
$0=\sum_n f(n)-\pi\coth(\pi a)/a$, that is,
\[
 \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2+a^2}=\frac{\pi}{a}\coth(\pi a).
\]
\textbf{Check.} As $a\to0$ the left side is $1/a^2+2\sum_{n\ge1}1/n^2+\cdots$. Since
$\coth x=(1+x^2/2+\cdots)/(x+x^3/6+\cdots)=1/x+x/3+\cdots$ (pull out $1/x$, then $1/(1+x^2/6)\approx1-x^2/6$), the right side
is $1/a^2+\pi^2/3+\cdots$. Hence $\sum_{n\ge1}1/n^2=\pi^2/6$, the Basel value used in
Chapters~2 and~7.

\textbf{Other sample sets.} The same residue count with \eqref{eq:res-ratio} gives:
\begin{itemize}
 \item $\pi\csc\pi z=\pi/\sin\pi z$ has residue $\pi/(\pi\cos n\pi)=(-1)^n$ at $z=n$, so
       it samples $\sum(-1)^m f(m)$;
 \item $\pi/\cos\pi z$ has residue $\pi/\bigl(-\pi\sin(\pi n+\tfrac\pi2)\bigr)=(-1)^{n+1}$
       at $z=n+\tfrac12$, so it samples $\sum(-1)^{n+1}f(n+\tfrac12)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{The steepest-descent approximation}
\footnotesize

\textbf{Step 1: set up the large-parameter integral.}
Many asymptotic formulas for special functions come from integrals of the form
\begin{equation}
  I(s)=\int_\Gamma g(z)\,e^{s f(z)}\,dz,
  \qquad s\to+\infty,
  \label{eq:sd-int}
\end{equation}
where $f$ and $g$ are holomorphic near $\Gamma$, so $\Gamma$ may be deformed as long as it
crosses no singularity.  Write $f=u+iv$ with real $u,v$.  As $s$ grows,
$e^{su}$ favors points where $u$ is largest on the deformed contour, so for large $s$ only
the neighbourhood of that highest point matters (we assume it is not an endpoint).

\textbf{Step 2: locate a nondegenerate saddle.}
We seek $z_0$ with $f'(z_0)=0$, $f''(z_0)\ne0$.
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.62\linewidth}
Since $f'=u_x+iv_x=u_x-iu_y$ by \eqref{eq:CR}, $z_0$ is a critical point of $u$. It
cannot be a hilltop: $u_{xx}=v_{yx}=-u_{yy}$, so if $u$ curves down along $x$ it curves up
along $y$. Critical points of $u$ are therefore mountain passes.

Picture $u$ as terrain (right). A contour between two valleys must cross the ridge; the
best crossing is the pass $z_0$ (the route's highest point is lowest), and from there the
steepest-descent path runs down into both valleys.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.34\linewidth}
\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=0.42\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figs/saddle-level-curves.pdf}
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\framebreak
\textbf{Step 3: choose the descent direction.}
By Taylor's theorem, with no linear term because $f'(z_0)=0$,
\begin{equation}
  f(z)=f(z_0)+\frac12 f''(z_0)(z-z_0)^2+O\bigl((z-z_0)^3\bigr).
  \label{eq:saddle-taylor}
\end{equation}
With $f''(z_0)=|f''(z_0)|e^{i\theta}$ and $z-z_0=re^{i\phi}$, the quadratic term is
\[
  \frac12 f''(z_0)(z-z_0)^2
  =\frac12|f''(z_0)|r^2\,e^{i(\theta+2\phi)}.
\]
Its real part $\tfrac12|f''(z_0)|r^2\cos(\theta+2\phi)$ decreases fastest when
$\cos(\theta+2\phi)=-1$, and there the imaginary part $\tfrac12|f''(z_0)|r^2\sin(\theta+2\phi)$
is zero: along this \emph{steepest-descent} direction the integrand does not oscillate,
so nothing cancels. Hence
\begin{equation}
  \theta+2\phi=\pi\pmod{2\pi},
  \qquad\text{that is}\qquad
  \phi=\frac{\pi-\theta}{2}\pmod{\pi}.
  \label{eq:descent-direction}
\end{equation}
The ambiguity by $\pi$ gives the two opposite rays leaving $z_0$; Step~4 uses both by
letting $r$ run over all of $\mathbb R$. Along them
$\frac12 f''(z_0)(z-z_0)^2=-\frac12|f''(z_0)|r^2$.
(The orthogonal choice $\theta+2\phi=0$ is steepest \emph{ascent}.)

\textbf{Step 4: reduce to a Gaussian integral.}
Deform the contour to pass through $z_0$ along both descent rays (assume $g(z_0)\ne0$).
Parameterize the full descent line by
\[
  \alpha=e^{i(\pi-\theta)/2}=i\,e^{-i\theta/2},
  \qquad
  z=z_0+\alpha r,
  \qquad
  r\in\mathbb R,
\]
so $dz=\alpha\,dr$, and $\alpha^2=-e^{-i\theta}$ gives $f''(z_0)\alpha^2=-|f''(z_0)|$.


\framebreak
\emph{Why only a small window matters.} Along this line \eqref{eq:saddle-taylor} gives
$e^{sf}\approx e^{sf(z_0)}\exp(-\frac12 s|f''(z_0)|r^2)$. This factor is negligible once $|r|$ is several times $1/\sqrt{s|f''(z_0)|}$, a window that shrinks as $s$
grows. Inside it the cubic term is $s\cdot O(r^3)=O(s^{-1/2})$ and $g\approx g(z_0)$.
So replace $f$ by \eqref{eq:saddle-taylor},
$g$ by $g(z_0)$, and extend the limits to $\pm\infty$ ($A\sim B$ means $A/B\to1$):
\begin{align*}
  I(s)
  &\sim \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(z_0)
    \exp\Bigl(sf(z_0)+\frac12 s f''(z_0)\alpha^2r^2\Bigr)\alpha\,dr\\
  &=g(z_0)e^{sf(z_0)}\alpha
    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
    \exp\Bigl(-\frac12 s|f''(z_0)|r^2\Bigr)\,dr
    &&\bigl(f''(z_0)\alpha^2=-|f''(z_0)|\bigr).
\end{align*}
The Gaussian integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ar^2}\,dr=\sqrt{\pi/a}$ ($a=1$ is proved in Chapter~2; set $x=\sqrt a\,r$) with
$a=\tfrac12 s|f''(z_0)|$, and $\alpha=i\,e^{-i\theta/2}$, give
\begin{equation}
  I(s)\sim
  g(z_0)e^{sf(z_0)}\,i\,e^{-i\theta/2}
  \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{s|f''(z_0)|}}.
  \label{eq:sd-result}
\end{equation}
Traversing the line in the opposite direction flips the sign; several equally high
saddles add their contributions. An endpoint maximum (excluded in Step~1) gives a smaller
term, $\int_0^\infty g(t)e^{-st}dt\sim g(0)/s$ by one integration by parts: $1/s$, not $1/\sqrt s$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Real special cases: Laplace method and stationary phase}
\footnotesize

The same local-Gaussian idea appears in two real-variable forms used later in the course.

\textbf{Laplace's method (real, decaying exponential).}
If the real function $\varphi$ has a single interior maximum at $x_0$
($\varphi'(x_0)=0$, $\varphi''(x_0)<0$) and $g(x_0)\ne0$,
\begin{equation}
  \int_a^b g(x)\,e^{s\varphi(x)}\,dx
  \sim
  g(x_0)e^{s\varphi(x_0)}
  \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{s\bigl|\varphi''(x_0)\bigr|}},
  \qquad s\to+\infty.
  \label{eq:laplace-real}
\end{equation}
This is \eqref{eq:sd-result} on the real line with $f=\varphi$ and $\theta=\arg\varphi''(x_0)=\pi$,
so $\alpha=i\,e^{-i\pi/2}=1$: the real axis is already the descent line. Chapter~2 uses it
for Stirling's approximation of $n!$ from the gamma integral.

\textbf{Stationary phase (real, oscillatory).}
Now the exponential does not decay but spins: away from a point where $\varphi'=0$ the
phase $s\varphi$ changes fast, so $e^{is\varphi}$ runs around the unit circle and
neighbouring pieces cancel. Only near a \emph{stationary point} $x_0$, where the phase is
momentarily flat, do the pieces add up---the same reason light follows the path of
stationary optical length. For a single stationary point $x_0$ inside $(a,b)$ with
$\varphi''(x_0)\ne0$, $g(x_0)\ne0$ (several such points add), and $\operatorname{sgn}q=\pm1$
the sign of $q$,
\begin{equation}
  \int_a^b g(x)\,e^{is\varphi(x)}\,dx
  \sim
  g(x_0)e^{is\varphi(x_0)}
  e^{i(\pi/4)\,\operatorname{sgn}\varphi''(x_0)}
  \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{s\bigl|\varphi''(x_0)\bigr|}},
  \qquad s\to+\infty.
  \label{eq:stationary-phase}
\end{equation}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Where the stationary-phase factor $e^{\pm i\pi/4}$ comes from}
\footnotesize

We derive \eqref{eq:stationary-phase}; its only new ingredient is the phase $e^{\pm i\pi/4}$.
Expand the phase near the stationary point $x_0$:
\[
  \varphi(x)\approx\varphi(x_0)+\tfrac12\varphi''(x_0)\,(x-x_0)^2.
\]
Set $u=\sqrt s\,(x-x_0)$, so $dx=du/\sqrt s$ and
$s\cdot\tfrac12\varphi''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2=\tfrac12\varphi''(x_0)\,u^2$.  Only the window
$|x-x_0|\lesssim1/\sqrt{s|\varphi''(x_0)|}$ contributes; outside it the phase
spins and the pieces cancel. So replace $g$ by $g(x_0)$ and let the $u$-limits run to $\pm\infty$:
\[
  \int_a^b g(x)\,e^{is\varphi(x)}\,dx
  \sim
  e^{is\varphi(x_0)}\,\frac{g(x_0)}{\sqrt s}
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
    e^{i\,(\varphi''(x_0)/2)\,u^2}\,du.
\]
The last integral is a Gaussian with an imaginary coefficient. Setting $c=\mp ia$ in
$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-cu^2}\,du=\sqrt{\pi/c}$ and taking the principal root,
$\sqrt{1/(\mp i)}=\sqrt{\pm i}=e^{\pm i\pi/4}$, gives the Fresnel formula
\[
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\pm iau^2}\,du
 =\sqrt{\frac\pi a}\,e^{\pm i\pi/4},\qquad a>0
\]
(we take on trust that the Gaussian formula holds for imaginary $c$).
Apply it with $a=\tfrac12|\varphi''(x_0)|$ and the sign $\pm$ equal to
$\operatorname{sgn}\varphi''(x_0)$: then
$\frac{g(x_0)}{\sqrt s}\sqrt{\pi/a}=g(x_0)\sqrt{2\pi/(s|\varphi''(x_0)|)}$, and the phase
factor is $e^{i(\pi/4)\operatorname{sgn}\varphi''(x_0)}$. This is \eqref{eq:stationary-phase}.
Chapter~3 applies this rule to the Airy integral at large negative argument.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Worked example: large binomial coefficient}
\footnotesize

Let $m,n$ be integers with $0<m<n$. As $n\to\infty$, keep $t=m/n$ away from $0$ and $1$. From
$(1+z)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk z^k$, Cauchy's coefficient formula \eqref{eq:laurent-coef}
(expansion center $0$, $k=m$, on any counterclockwise circle about $0$) picks out the
coefficient $\binom nm$ of $z^m$:
\begin{align*}
  \binom nm
  &=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint\frac{(1+z)^n}{z^{m+1}}\,dz\\
  &=\oint\frac{1}{2\pi i z}\,e^{n\log(1+z)-nt\log z}\,dz
  =\oint g(z)e^{nf(z)}\,dz,
\end{align*}
where we wrote $(1+z)^n=e^{n\log(1+z)}$ and $z^{-m}=e^{-m\log z}=e^{-nt\log z}$ (since
$m=nt$), so that
\[
  g(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i z},
  \qquad
  f(z)=\log(1+z)-t\log z
\]
(with branches chosen locally near the positive saddle below).

\textbf{Saddle.}  Differentiate:
\[
  f'(z)=\frac1{1+z}-\frac t z,
  \qquad
  f''(z)=-\frac1{(1+z)^2}+\frac t{z^2}.
\]
Set $f'(z_0)=0$:
\[
  z_0-t(1+z_0)=0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  z_0=\frac{t}{1-t}=\frac{m}{n-m}>0.
\]
Then $1+z_0=1/(1-t)$, and
\begin{align*}
  f(z_0)
  &=\log\frac1{1-t}-t\log\frac{t}{1-t}
   =-\log(1-t)-t\log t+t\log(1-t)\\
  &=-t\log t-(1-t)\log(1-t),\\
  f''(z_0)
  &=-\frac1{(1+z_0)^2}+\frac{t}{z_0^2}
   =-(1-t)^2+\frac{(1-t)^2}{t}\\
  &=(1-t)^2\Bigl(\frac1t-1\Bigr)
   =\frac{(1-t)^3}{t}>0.
\end{align*}
Thus $\theta=\arg f''(z_0)=0$, so the descent direction from \eqref{eq:descent-direction} is
$\phi=\pi/2$: the local descent line is vertical.  Take the contour to be the circle $|z|=z_0$, and write $z=z_0e^{i\psi}$ on it.  Since
$|g(z)e^{nf(z)}|=|1+z|^n/(2\pi|z|^{m+1})$ and $|z|=z_0$ is fixed, the integrand is largest where
\[
 |1+z_0e^{i\psi}|^2=(1+z_0\cos\psi)^2+z_0^2\sin^2\psi=1+2z_0\cos\psi+z_0^2
\]
is largest, namely at $\psi=0$. The saddle $z=z_0$ therefore dominates.
The counterclockwise tangent there points upward ($+i$), matching the descent
direction and fixing the sign of the result.

\textbf{Apply \eqref{eq:sd-result} with $s=n$.} Since $\theta=0$ and $f''(z_0)>0$,
$e^{-i\theta/2}=1$ and $|f''(z_0)|=f''(z_0)$; the factor $i$ then cancels the $i$ in
$g(z_0)=1/(2\pi i z_0)$:
\begin{align*}
  \binom nm
  &\sim
  \frac{1}{2\pi i z_0}\,e^{nf(z_0)}\,i
  \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{n f''(z_0)}}
  =
  \frac{1}{2\pi z_0}\,e^{nf(z_0)}
  \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{n f''(z_0)}}.
\end{align*}
Substitute $1/z_0=(1-t)/t$ and $1/f''(z_0)=t/(1-t)^3$:
\[
  \frac{1}{2\pi z_0}
  \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{n f''(z_0)}}
  =\frac{1-t}{2\pi t}
    \sqrt{\frac{2\pi t}{n(1-t)^3}}
  =\sqrt{\frac{(1-t)^2}{(2\pi t)^2}\cdot\frac{2\pi t}{n(1-t)^3}}
  =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n t(1-t)}}.
\]
Also $e^{nf(z_0)}=\exp\bigl[-n\bigl(t\log t+(1-t)\log(1-t)\bigr)\bigr]$, so
\begin{equation}
  \binom nm
  \sim
  \frac{\exp\bigl[-n\bigl(t\log t+(1-t)\log(1-t)\bigr)\bigr]}
       {\sqrt{2\pi n t(1-t)}}.
  \label{eq:binom-sd}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Check.} $n=20$, $m=10$: \eqref{eq:binom-sd} gives $2^{20}/\sqrt{10\pi}\approx187079$,
exact $184756$ (1.3\% high). Stirling's formula (Chapter~2) gives the same result.

\textbf{Meaning.} $H(t)=-t\log t-(1-t)\log(1-t)$ is the entropy of a coin with
heads-probability $t$. Multiplying \eqref{eq:binom-sd} by $t^m(1-t)^{n-m}=e^{-nH(t)}$ gives
the probability of exactly $m$ heads, $\approx1/\sqrt{2\pi nt(1-t)}$, the peak of a Gaussian
with the binomial variance $nt(1-t)$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Summary}
\footnotesize

\begin{itemize}
 \item Complex differentiability requires one direction-independent difference-quotient
       limit.  With continuous first partial derivatives, this is equivalent to the
       Cauchy--Riemann equations $u_x=v_y$, $u_y=-v_x$.
 \item Cauchy's theorem: a holomorphic $f$ integrates to zero around a closed contour, so
       contours may be deformed freely through regions where $f$ is holomorphic.  Cauchy's
       integral formula yields all derivatives and the local Taylor expansion.
 \item A Laurent series is valid on an annulus.  Its principal part classifies an isolated
       singularity as removable, a pole, or essential.  Branch points are not isolated and
       do not possess ordinary residues.
\framebreak
 \item The residue is the coefficient $a_{-1}$.  The residue theorem evaluates a closed
       contour integral as $2\pi i$ times the sum of enclosed residues.
 \item Three real-integral templates: semicircle (rational/exponential), keyhole
       (powers and branch cuts, as in $\int_0^\infty x^a/(1+x)^2\,dx$), and Jordan's
       indented semicircle (oscillatory integrals such as $\int\sin x/x\,dx$).  Always
       check orientation and vanishing arcs before taking limits.
 \item \textbf{Steepest descent} \eqref{eq:sd-result} is a local Gaussian approximation at a
       nondegenerate dominant saddle.  Real special cases: Laplace method
       \eqref{eq:laplace-real} (Stirling in Ch.~2) and stationary phase
       \eqref{eq:stationary-phase} (Airy in Ch.~3); large-$x$ Bessel (Ch.~5) uses the complex form.  Orientation and the phase
       of the square root are part of the answer.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
