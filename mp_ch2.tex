\begin{frame}{Where we are going}
\footnotesize
Many special functions begin as discrete objects---factorials, partial sums,
\(p\)-series---and gain power once they are extended to continuous (or complex)
arguments. This chapter develops the functions that form the basic
\emph{language} of that extension:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Harmonic numbers} \(H_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\tfrac1k\) and the continuous
        interpolation that produces the Euler--Mascheroni constant \(\gamma\).
  \item The \textbf{gamma function} \(\Gamma(z)\): the canonical meromorphic
        interpolation of the shifted factorial. Integral definition, recursion,
        half-integers, poles, reflection, and Stirling's formula.
  \item The \textbf{beta function} \(B(p,q)\): the integral that encodes binomial
        structure, linked to gamma by \(B(p,q)=\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)/\Gamma(p+q)\).
  \item \textbf{Riemann zeta function}
        \(\zeta(s)\) for \(\operatorname{Re}s>1\): series, integral representation,
        and the Basel value \(\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6\).
\end{itemize}
The common question is how to extend a discrete pattern to a function we can
differentiate, integrate, and use in later chapters. Write \(\mathbb N=\{1,2,\dots\}\) and \(\mathbb N_0=\{0,1,2,\dots\}\).
\end{frame}

\section{Harmonic Numbers}
% ======================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Block stacking and the harmonic numbers}
\footnotesize

\textbf{The question.} We stack \(n\) identical blocks at the edge of a table. Each block has
length \(2\) and mass \(1\). How far can the top block stick out past the edge without the
stack falling? Call this largest overhang \(H_n\). Put the table edge at \(x=0\), with the
table at \(x<0\).

\textbf{The balance rule.} A stack does not tip as long as its center of mass is not past the
edge. The best we can do is to put the center of mass exactly at the edge, \(x=0\).

\textbf{One block.} Its center of mass is its midpoint. Put the midpoint at \(x=0\): the block
sticks out by half its length, so \(H_1=1\).

\textbf{From \(n-1\) blocks to \(n\) blocks.} Suppose \(n-1\) blocks are already stacked with
their center of mass at the edge and overhang \(H_{n-1}\).
\begin{itemize}
  \item Slide one new block underneath, with its right end at the edge. Its center is then at
        \(x=-1\). The old stack now rests on this block, still balanced.
  \item The center of mass \(x\) of all \(n\) blocks is the mass-weighted average of the
        positions. The old stack has mass \(n-1\) at \(0\); the new block has mass \(1\) at \(-1\):
        \[
          n\,x = (n-1)\cdot 0 + 1\cdot(-1)
          \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
          x = -\frac1n .
        \]
  \item So the center of mass is now \(1/n\) behind the edge. Slide the whole stack forward by
        \(1/n\). The center of mass is back at the edge, and the overhang grew by \(1/n\).
\end{itemize}
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Result.} Each new block adds \(1/n\) to the overhang:
\[
  H_n=H_{n-1}+\frac1n .
\]
Starting from \(H_0=0\) and adding up the increments,
\begin{equation}
  H_n = 1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac14 + \cdots + \tfrac1n
      = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1k .
  \label{eq:harmonic}
\end{equation}
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Names.} The infinite series \(1+\tfrac12+\tfrac13+\cdots\) is the \emph{harmonic series}.
Its partial sums \(H_n\) are the \emph{harmonic numbers}:
\[
  H_1=1,\quad H_2=\tfrac32,\quad H_3=\tfrac{11}{6},\quad H_4=\tfrac{25}{12},\quad\dots
\]
\textbf{They grow without bound, but very slowly.} We will show below that
\(H_n\approx\log n+0.577\). For example, an overhang of \(50\) block lengths needs \(H_n>100\), which
takes about \(1.5\times10^{43}\) blocks.

This raises two questions we now answer.
\begin{itemize}
  \item What natural analytic function interpolates the discrete sequence \(H_n\)?
  \item How slowly does the sequence diverge?
\end{itemize}
The harmonic series is the special case \(p=1\) of the \(p\)-series
\(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{-p}\), which reappears as the zeta function at the end of this chapter.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Interpolating the partial sums}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Goal.} \(H_n\) is defined only for whole numbers \(n\). We look for a smooth function
\(f(s)\) of a continuous variable \(s\) with \(f(n)=H_n\). The trick is to write \(H_n\) as an
integral in which \(n\) appears only as a parameter.

\textbf{Step 1: a finite sum as an integral.} The geometric sum formula gives
\[
 \frac{1-x^n}{1-x}=1+x+x^2+\cdots+x^{n-1}\qquad(x\neq1).
\]
(At \(x=1\) the left side is \(0/0\), but the right side is \(n\); so the integrand below has no
real problem at \(x=1\).) Integrate both sides from \(0\) to \(1\). Each power gives
\(\int_0^1x^k\,dx=1/(k+1)\), so
\[
 \int_0^1\frac{1-x^n}{1-x}\,dx
 =1+\frac12+\cdots+\frac1n=H_n .
\]

\textbf{Step 2: let \(n\) be any number.} The left side makes sense even when \(n\) is not a whole
number. So define
\begin{equation}
 f(s)=\int_0^1\frac{1-x^s}{1-x}\,dx,\qquad \operatorname{Re}s>-1.
 \label{eq:harmonic-interp}
\end{equation}
By Step~1, \(f(n)=H_n\) for every whole number \(n\).
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Why \(\operatorname{Re}s>-1\)?} Here \(x^s=e^{s\log x}\), so \(|x^s|=x^{\operatorname{Re}s}\) on
\(0<x<1\). The integral can fail only at the two endpoints.
\begin{itemize}
  \item At \(x=0\): the term \(x^s\) behaves like \(x^{\operatorname{Re}s}\), and
        \(\int_0 x^\sigma\,dx\) is finite exactly when \(\sigma>-1\).
  \item At \(x=1\): by l'H\^opital's rule \((1-x^s)/(1-x)\to s\), a finite value, so there is no
        problem.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Step 3: the recursion still holds.} The block-stacking rule was
\(H_n=H_{n-1}+1/n\). Does \(f\) obey the same rule, \(f(s+1)=f(s)+\frac1{s+1}\)? Subtract the integrand at
\(s\) from the integrand at \(s+1\):
\begin{align*}
 f(s+1)-f(s)&=\int_0^1\frac{(1-x^{s+1})-(1-x^s)}{1-x}\,dx
 =\int_0^1\frac{x^s(1-x)}{1-x}\,dx\\
 &=\int_0^1x^s\,dx=\frac1{s+1},\qquad f(0)=\int_0^1\frac{1-1}{1-x}\,dx=0.
\end{align*}
So \(f\) answers the first question: it is a smooth ``\(H_s\)'' for every \(s\) with \(\operatorname{Re}s>-1\), and it obeys
the same recursion as \(H_n\).

\textbf{Example: \(s=\tfrac12\).} Substitute \(x=u^2\), so \(dx=2u\,du\), and use
\((1-u)/(1-u^2)=1/(1+u)\):
\[
 f\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)=\int_0^1\frac{1-u}{1-u^2}\,2u\,du=2\int_0^1\Bigl(1-\frac1{1+u}\Bigr)du
 =2-2\log2\approx0.614,
\]
which lies between \(H_0=0\) and \(H_1=1\), as it should.

\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Second question: how fast does \(H_n\) grow?} We compare \(H_n\) with
\(\log n=\int_1^n dx/x\), which is also a sum of \(1/x\), but a continuous one.

\textbf{Step 1: \(H_n\) is larger than \(\log n\).} Write each term \(1/k\) as the area of a
rectangle of width \(1\) and height \(1/k\) over \([k,k+1]\): \(\int_k^{k+1}dx/k=1/k\). On that
interval the curve \(1/x\) lies below the rectangle's top, because \(1/x<1/k\) for \(x>k\). So each
rectangle has more area than the region under the curve:
\[
 H_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_k^{k+1}\frac{dx}{k}
 \;>\;\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_k^{k+1}\frac{dx}{x}
 =\int_1^{n+1}\frac{dx}{x}=\log(n+1)\;>\;\log n.
\]
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 2: the gap \(a_n=H_n-\log n\) shrinks.} Going from \(n\) to \(n+1\), \(H_n\) gains
\(1/(n+1)\) while \(\log n\) gains \(\log(n+1)-\log n=\int_n^{n+1}dx/x\). Therefore
\[
 a_{n+1}-a_n=\frac1{n+1}-\int_n^{n+1}\frac{dx}{x}
 =\int_n^{n+1}\Bigl(\frac1{n+1}-\frac1x\Bigr)dx<0,
\]
because \(1/x>1/(n+1)\) for \(n<x<n+1\). So \(a_1>a_2>a_3>\cdots\).

\textbf{Step 3: the gap has a limit.} By Step~1, every \(a_n\) is positive. A decreasing sequence
that stays above \(0\) must converge. Its limit is the Euler--Mascheroni constant (its numerical value
is derived right after the next picture):
\begin{equation}
 \gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}(H_n-\log n)=0.577215664901532\ldots.
 \label{eq:euler-gamma}
\end{equation}
In words: for large \(n\), \(H_n\approx\log n+\gamma\). The harmonic numbers grow like a
logarithm, which is why the overhang grows so slowly.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Euler--Mascheroni as a staircase error}
\footnotesize
\begin{center}
  \includegraphics[width=0.68\linewidth]{figs/ch2_euler_gamma_staircase.pdf}
\end{center}
The boxes of Step~1 (height \(1/k\) over \([k,k+1]\)) have total area \(H_n\); the curve \(1/x\) lies
below them, with area \(\log(n+1)\) from \(1\) to \(n+1\). The shaded slivers between them add up to
\(H_n-\log(n+1)\), which tends to \(\gamma\) because \(\log(n+1)-\log n=\log(1+\frac1n)\to0\).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Computing $\gamma$: trapping it between two bounds}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Goal.} Step~3 showed that \(\gamma\) exists. Now find its value. Since \(a_n=H_n-\log n\) decreases to \(\gamma\),
every \(a_n\) is an upper bound: \(\gamma<a_n\). We also need a lower bound.

\textbf{Step 1: the distance to the limit is a sum of drops.} Since \(a_k\to\gamma\),
\[
 a_n-\gamma=(a_n-a_{n+1})+(a_{n+1}-a_{n+2})+\cdots=\sum_{k\ge n}(a_k-a_{k+1}).
\]
By Step~2 of the growth argument above (``the gap shrinks''), each drop is an area,
\(a_k-a_{k+1}=\int_k^{k+1}\bigl(\frac1x-\frac1{k+1}\bigr)dx\): the region between the curve \(1/x\) and the horizontal
line at height \(1/(k+1)\).

\textbf{Step 2: compare each drop with a triangle.} The chord from \((k,\frac1k)\) to \((k+1,\frac1{k+1})\) lies above
the curve, because \(1/x\) bends upward (its second derivative \(2/x^3\) is positive). So the region is
smaller than the triangle below the chord and above height \(\frac1{k+1}\), which has base \(1\) and height
\(\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}\):
\[
 0<a_k-a_{k+1}<\frac12\Bigl(\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}\Bigr).
\]
\textbf{Step 3: add up.} The triangle areas telescope:
\(\sum_{k\ge n}\frac12\bigl(\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}\bigr)=\frac12\bigl(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}+\frac1{n+1}-\frac1{n+2}+\cdots\bigr)=\frac1{2n}\). So
\(0<a_n-\gamma<\frac1{2n}\), that is,
\begin{equation}
 H_n-\log n-\frac1{2n}<\gamma<H_n-\log n .
 \label{eq:gamma-bounds}
\end{equation}
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 4: compute.} For \(n=10\): \(H_{10}=\frac{7381}{2520}=2.928968\) and \(\log10=2.302585\), so
\(a_{10}=0.626383\) and
\[
 0.576383<\gamma<0.626383 .
\]
The lower bound is much closer: over a short interval the curve is nearly straight, so the triangle
is a good approximation and \(\gamma\approx H_n-\log n-\frac1{2n}\). For \(n=100\): \(H_{100}-\log100=0.582207\), and
subtracting \(\frac1{200}=0.005\) gives \(\gamma\approx0.577207\). Larger \(n\) (by computer) gives
\(\gamma=0.5772156649\ldots\), the value in \eqref{eq:euler-gamma}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Definition and the factorial recursion}
\footnotesize

\textbf{Goal.} Find a smooth function that equals \(n!\) at the whole numbers. We use the same
idea as for \(H_n\): write \(n!\) as an integral in which \(n\) is only a parameter, then let
\(n\) be any number.

\textbf{Step 1: which integral gives \(n!\)?} Look at \(\int_0^\infty t^ne^{-t}\,dt\).
Integration by parts moves a derivative onto \(t^n\), which lowers the power by one and brings
down a factor \(n\). The factor \(e^{-t}\) is the right partner because it stays \(e^{-t}\) (up to
sign) when integrated, and it kills the boundary terms at \(t=\infty\). One step gives
\[
 \int_0^\infty t^ne^{-t}\,dt=n\int_0^\infty t^{n-1}e^{-t}\,dt .
\]
Repeating it \(n\) times leaves \(n(n-1)\cdots1\cdot\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,dt=n!\). (Steps~3--4 below
carry this out carefully.)

\textbf{Step 2: define \(\Gamma\).} The integral still makes sense when \(n\) is not a whole number.
By a historical convention we write \(z-1\) in place of \(n\). This defines the \emph{gamma
function} (Euler's integral):
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma(z)=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}t^{z-1}\,dt,
  \qquad \operatorname{Re}z>0.
  \label{eq:gamma-def}
\end{equation}
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Why \(\operatorname{Re}z>0\)?} Write \(\sigma=\operatorname{Re}z\), so
\(|t^{z-1}|=t^{\sigma-1}\).
\begin{itemize}
  \item Near \(t=0\): \(e^{-t}\approx1\), and \(\int_0 t^{\sigma-1}\,dt\) is finite exactly when
        \(\sigma>0\).
  \item Near \(t=\infty\): \(e^{-t}\) decays faster than any power of \(t\) grows, so there is no
        problem.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Why this function and not another?} Many smooth functions pass through the points
\((n+1,\,n!)\). \(\Gamma\) is the standard choice because of a uniqueness theorem (Bohr--Mollerup,
quoted): it is the only positive function on \(x>0\) with \(f(1)=1\), \(f(x+1)=xf(x)\), and
\(\log f\) convex.

\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 3: derive the recursion.}  Integrate \(\Gamma(z+1)\) by parts with \(u=t^z\),
\(dv=e^{-t}dt\), so \(du=z\,t^{z-1}dt\) and \(v=-e^{-t}\):
\[
  \Gamma(z+1)
   =\int_0^\infty e^{-t}t^z\,dt
   =\left[-t^ze^{-t}\right]_{0}^{\infty}
     +z\int_0^\infty e^{-t}t^{z-1}\,dt.
\]
The boundary term is zero: \(|t^ze^{-t}|=t^\sigma e^{-t}\) tends to \(0\) at \(t=0\) (because
\(\sigma>0\)) and at \(t=\infty\) (because \(e^{-t}\) wins). The remaining integral is \(\Gamma(z)\).
Therefore
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma(z+1)=z\,\Gamma(z).
  \label{eq:gamma-rec}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 4: recover factorials.}  First the starting value:
\(\Gamma(1)=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,dt=\left[-e^{-t}\right]_{0}^{\infty}=1\).
Then apply \eqref{eq:gamma-rec} again and again, lowering the argument by one each time:
\[
  \Gamma(n+1)=n\,\Gamma(n)=n(n-1)\Gamma(n-1)=\cdots=n(n-1)\cdots2\cdot1\,\Gamma(1)=n!.
\]
Together with \(\Gamma(1)=1=0!\), this proves
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma(n+1)=n!,
  \qquad n=0,1,2,\dots .
  \label{eq:gamma-fact}
\end{equation}
Keep track of the shift: \(\Gamma(n+1)=n!\), not \(\Gamma(n)=n!\).
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Worked examples: half-integer values}
\footnotesize

\textbf{Goal.} Compute \(\Gamma(\tfrac12),\Gamma(\tfrac32),\Gamma(\tfrac52),\dots\) exactly.
These values appear later in the Bessel functions of Chapters~3 and~5.

\textbf{Plan.} The recursion \(\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)\) reduces every \(\Gamma(m+\tfrac12)\) to the
single number \(\Gamma(\tfrac12)\). So we need only \(\Gamma(\tfrac12)\), and it turns out to be the
Gaussian integral \(\int e^{-x^2}dx\) of Chapter~1.

\textbf{Step 1: recall the Gaussian integral} from Chapter~1 (computed there by squaring it and using
polar coordinates):
\begin{equation}
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx=\sqrt\pi .
  \label{eq:gauss-int}
\end{equation}

\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 2: turn \(\Gamma\) into a Gaussian integral.} The integrand of \(\Gamma\) contains
\(e^{-t}\); to get \(e^{-u^2}\) we substitute \(t=u^2\) in \eqref{eq:gamma-def}. Then
\(dt=2u\,du\), \(t^{z-1}=u^{2z-2}\), and \(t:0\to\infty\) becomes \(u:0\to\infty\):
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma(z)=\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2}u^{2z-2}(2u)\,du
  =2\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2}u^{2z-1}\,du,
  \qquad \operatorname{Re}z>0.
  \label{eq:gamma-gauss}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Step 3: set \(z=\tfrac12\).} Then \(u^{2z-1}=u^0=1\). Since \(e^{-u^2}\) is even, twice the
integral over \((0,\infty)\) is the integral over the whole line, which is \eqref{eq:gauss-int}:
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma\left(\tfrac12\right)=2\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2}\,du
  =\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-u^2}\,du=\sqrt\pi.
  \label{eq:gamma-half}
\end{equation}
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 4: climb with the recursion.} Use \(\Gamma(z+1)=z\,\Gamma(z)\) repeatedly:
\[
 \Gamma\bigl(\tfrac32\bigr)=\tfrac12\,\Gamma\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2},\qquad
 \Gamma\bigl(\tfrac52\bigr)=\tfrac32\,\Gamma\bigl(\tfrac32\bigr)=\frac{3\sqrt\pi}{4},\qquad
 \Gamma\bigl(\tfrac72\bigr)=\tfrac52\,\Gamma\bigl(\tfrac52\bigr)=\frac{15\sqrt\pi}{8}.
\]
The pattern: after \(m\) steps we have multiplied by \(m\) factors \(\tfrac12,\tfrac32,\dots,\tfrac{2m-1}2\),
each an odd number over \(2\):
\[
 \Gamma\left(m+\tfrac12\right)
 =\frac{1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2m-1)}{2^m}\,\sqrt\pi .
\]
\textbf{Step 5 (optional): a closed form.} The product of odd numbers can be written with
factorials. Split \((2m)!\) into its even and its odd factors. The even factors are
\(2\cdot4\cdots(2m)=2^m\,m!\). Hence
\[
 (2m)!=2^m\,m!\cdot\bigl[1\cdot3\cdots(2m-1)\bigr]
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 1\cdot3\cdots(2m-1)=\frac{(2m)!}{2^m\,m!},
\]
and therefore
\[
 \Gamma\left(m+\tfrac12\right)=\frac{(2m)!}{4^m\,m!}\sqrt\pi .
\]
Check \(m=2\): \(\frac{24}{16\cdot2}\sqrt\pi=\frac34\sqrt\pi\), as in Step~4. It also holds at \(m=0\).
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Analytic continuation to the left half-plane}
\footnotesize

\textbf{The problem.} The integral \eqref{eq:gamma-def} defines \(\Gamma(z)\) only for
\(\operatorname{Re}z>0\). But Bessel series (Chapters~3 and~5) need values such as
\(\Gamma(-\tfrac12)\), where the integral diverges. We need a definition that works there too.

\textbf{The idea: run the recursion backward.} Divide \(\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)\) by \(z\):
\[
  \Gamma(z)=\frac{\Gamma(z+1)}{z}.
\]
Read this as a \emph{definition} of the left side. Example: \(\Gamma(-\tfrac12)\) has no integral,
but the right side needs only \(\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi\), which we know:
\[
  \Gamma(-\tfrac12)=\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac12)}{-\tfrac12}=-2\sqrt\pi .
\]
In general, the right side is defined by the integral whenever \(\operatorname{Re}(z+1)>0\). So this
extends \(\Gamma\) to the strip \(-1<\operatorname{Re}z\le0\), except at \(z=0\), where we would divide by
zero.
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Going further left.} To reach \(\operatorname{Re}z>-2\), \(-3\), \dots, use the recursion
several times. Applying it \(m\) times gives
\begin{align*}
  \Gamma(z+m)
   &=(z+m-1)\Gamma(z+m-1)
   =(z+m-1)(z+m-2)\Gamma(z+m-2)\\
   &=\cdots
    =(z+m-1)(z+m-2)\cdots(z+1)z\,\Gamma(z).
\end{align*}
Solve for \(\Gamma(z)\). If we pick \(m\) large enough that \(\operatorname{Re}(z+m)>0\), the
numerator below is given by the integral, so the right side is known:
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma(z)
   =\frac{\Gamma(z+m)}
          {z(z+1)(z+2)\cdots(z+m-1)}.
  \label{eq:gamma-continue}
\end{equation}
This defines \(\Gamma\) everywhere except where a denominator factor is zero, that is, at
\(z=0,-1,-2,\dots\). (Choosing a larger \(m\) gives the same value, by the recursion, so the
definition is consistent.)
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{What happens at \(z=0,-1,-2,\dots\)?} Look near \(z=-n\) (\(n=0,1,2,\dots\)). Write
\(z=-n+\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon\) small, and use \eqref{eq:gamma-continue} with \(m=n+1\).
The last denominator factor is \(z+n=\varepsilon\):
\[
  \Gamma(-n+\varepsilon)
   =\frac{\Gamma(1+\varepsilon)}
     {(-n+\varepsilon)(-n+1+\varepsilon)\cdots(-1+\varepsilon)\cdot\varepsilon}.
\]
As \(\varepsilon\to0\):
\begin{itemize}
  \item the numerator tends to \(\Gamma(1)=1\);
  \item the first \(n\) denominator factors tend to \((-n)(-n+1)\cdots(-1)=(-1)^n\,n!\)
        (there are \(n\) negative factors; for \(n=0\) there are none, and the product is \(1\));
  \item the last factor \(\varepsilon\) tends to \(0\).
\end{itemize}
So, using \(1/(-1)^n=(-1)^n\),
\[
  \Gamma(-n+\varepsilon)\approx\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\qquad(\varepsilon\to0).
\]
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Two names for this behavior.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(\Gamma\) blows up like a constant times \(1/\varepsilon\), the first power of
        \(1/(\text{distance to }{-n})\). This is called a \emph{simple pole} (Chapter~1).
  \item The constant in front of \(1/\varepsilon\) is called the \emph{residue}. It is the number
        that matters in contour integrals: on a small circle around \(-n\), the term \(1/\varepsilon\)
        integrates to \(2\pi i\), while the rest (which has no blow-up) integrates to zero.
\end{itemize}
To read off the residue, multiply by \(\varepsilon=z+n\) and let \(\varepsilon\to0\):
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname*{Res}_{z=-n}\Gamma(z)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\varepsilon\,\Gamma(-n+\varepsilon)=\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}.
  \label{eq:gamma-residue}
\end{equation}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Real-axis picture of $\Gamma$}
\footnotesize
\begin{center}
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{figs/gamma-real.pdf}
\end{center}
\textbf{Right half:} \(\Gamma\) passes through \(\Gamma(n+1)=n!\) and grows very fast.
\textbf{Left half:} \(\Gamma\) blows up at each of \(0,-1,-2,\dots\) (the poles), and its sign
alternates between them. Why? Take \(x\) between \(-n\) and \(-n+1\), and use
\eqref{eq:gamma-continue} with \(m=n+1\):
\(\Gamma(x)=\Gamma(x+n+1)\big/\bigl[x(x+1)\cdots(x+n)\bigr]\).
The numerator is positive, since \(x+n+1\) lies in \((1,2)\). In the denominator, the \(n\) factors
\(x,\dots,x+n-1\) are negative and the last one is positive. So \(\Gamma(x)\) has the sign \((-1)^n\).
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Euler's reflection formula}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Why we want it.} So far, values of \(\Gamma\) on the left come only from dividing again
and again. The reflection formula relates \(\Gamma(z)\) to \(\Gamma(1-z)\) in one step. (The points
\(z\) and \(1-z\) are mirror images about \(\tfrac12\).) Chapter~5 uses it for the Bessel functions
of the second kind.
\begin{block}{Euler's reflection formula}
\vspace{-1.2ex}
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi z)},\qquad z\notin\mathbb Z.
  \label{eq:gamma-reflect}
\end{equation}
\end{block}
Check at \(z=\tfrac12\): \(\Gamma(\tfrac12)^2=\pi/\sin(\pi/2)=\pi\), in agreement with \eqref{eq:gamma-half}.

\textbf{Plan of the proof.} First prove it for real \(a\) with \(0<a<1\), where both
\(\Gamma(a)\) and \(\Gamma(1-a)\) are given by integrals. Then extend to all \(z\) by the
identity theorem.
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 1: write the product as a double integral.} Use \eqref{eq:gamma-def} twice, with
different integration variables \(t\) and \(s\):
\[
  \Gamma(a)=\int_0^\infty t^{a-1}e^{-t}\,dt,\qquad
  \Gamma(1-a)=\int_0^\infty s^{-a}e^{-s}\,ds,
\]
\[
  \Gamma(a)\Gamma(1-a)
   =\int_0^\infty\!\!\int_0^\infty
      t^{a-1}s^{-a}e^{-(t+s)}\,dt\,ds.
\]
\textbf{Step 2: substitute \(t=su\).} For each fixed \(s\), measure \(t\) in units of \(s\). The aim
is to make the powers of \(s\) cancel. With \(dt=s\,du\):
\[
  t^{a-1}s^{-a}\,dt=(su)^{a-1}s^{-a}\,s\,du=u^{a-1}\,du,
  \qquad
  e^{-(t+s)}=e^{-s(1+u)}.
\]
Now \(s\) appears only in the exponential, so do the \(s\)-integral first (the integrand is
positive, so the order of integration may be swapped):
\[
  \Gamma(a)\Gamma(1-a)
   =\int_0^\infty u^{a-1}
      \underbrace{\left(\int_0^\infty e^{-s(1+u)}\,ds\right)}_{=\,1/(1+u)}du
   =\int_0^\infty\frac{u^{a-1}}{1+u}\,du.
\]
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 3: use Chapter~1.} There a keyhole contour gave
\(\int_0^\infty x^{-b}(1+x)^{-1}dx=\pi/\sin(\pi b)\) for \(0<b<1\). Our integral is this with
\(b=1-a\) (then \(x^{-b}=x^{a-1}\)), and \(\sin(\pi-\pi a)=\sin(\pi a)\). So
\[
  \Gamma(a)\Gamma(1-a)=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi a)},\qquad 0<a<1.
\]

\textbf{Step 4: extend to all \(z\notin\mathbb Z\).} Both sides of \eqref{eq:gamma-reflect} are
analytic on \(\mathbb C\setminus\mathbb Z\) (the plane with the integers removed), and they agree
on the segment \((0,1)\). The identity theorem of Chapter~1 says: two analytic functions that
agree on a segment agree on their whole connected domain. So the formula holds at every
noninteger \(z\).
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Consequence 1: \(\Gamma\) is never zero.} At a noninteger \(z\), the product
\(\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)\) equals \(\pi/\sin(\pi z)\neq0\), so neither factor can be zero. At the
positive integers, \(\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!\neq0\). At \(0,-1,-2,\dots\), \(\Gamma\) has poles.
(We will need this fact: it means \(1/\Gamma\) never blows up.)

\textbf{Consequence 2: a formula for \(\Gamma(-z)\).} Replace \(z\) by \(-z\) in
\eqref{eq:gamma-reflect}. Since sine is odd, \(\sin(-\pi z)=-\sin(\pi z)\), so
\(\Gamma(-z)\Gamma(1+z)=-\pi/\sin(\pi z)\). Divide by \(\Gamma(1+z)\), which is not zero:
\begin{equation}
 \Gamma(-z)=-\frac{\pi}{\Gamma(z+1)\sin(\pi z)},\qquad z\notin\mathbb Z.
 \label{eq:gamma-neg}
\end{equation}
It computes \(\Gamma\) at negative arguments from positive ones: at \(z=\tfrac12\),
\(\Gamma(-\tfrac12)=-\pi/\Gamma(\tfrac32)=-2\sqrt\pi\approx-3.54\), negative as the picture shows on \((-1,0)\).
\end{frame}






\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Worked examples: gamma integrals}
\footnotesize

\textbf{How to use \(\Gamma\).} Many integrals become Euler's integral
\(\int_0^\infty e^{-t}t^{z-1}dt=\Gamma(z)\) after a substitution. The trick is to choose the
substitution that turns the exponent into \(-t\).

\textbf{Example 1.} Evaluate \(\int_0^\infty e^{-x^p}\,dx\) for \(p>0\).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Substitute} \(t=x^p\), to turn \(e^{-x^p}\) into \(e^{-t}\). Then \(x=t^{1/p}\) and
        \(dx=\frac1p\,t^{1/p-1}\,dt\). The limits \(x=0,\infty\) become \(t=0,\infty\).
  \item \emph{Recognize \(\Gamma\).} The integrand is \(e^{-t}t^{z-1}\) with \(z-1=\frac1p-1\), that is,
        \(z=\frac1p\).
  \item \emph{Tidy up} with the recursion: \(\Gamma(1+\frac1p)=\frac1p\,\Gamma(\frac1p)\).
\end{itemize}
\begin{equation}
  \int_0^\infty e^{-x^p}\,dx
   =\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,\frac1p\,t^{1/p-1}\,dt
   =\frac1p\,\Gamma\left(\frac1p\right)
   =\Gamma\left(1+\frac1p\right).
  \label{eq:exp-xp}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Check} at \(p=2\): \(\Gamma(\tfrac32)=\sqrt\pi/2\). This is \(\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}dx\),
half of the Gaussian integral \eqref{eq:gauss-int}, as it should be.
\par\framebreak\par
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.68\textwidth}
\textbf{Example 2.} For \(p>1\), evaluate \(\int_0^\infty\cos(x^p)\,dx\).

\textbf{Idea.} Write \(\cos(x^p)=\operatorname{Re}e^{ix^p}\) and compute
\(\int_0^\infty e^{ix^p}dx\). On the real axis, \(e^{ix^p}\) has modulus \(1\) and only oscillates.
But along the ray at angle \(\pi/(2p)\) it becomes \(e^{-r^p}\), which decays, and Example~1 can
do that integral. So we rotate the path from the real axis to that ray, using the closed
sector in the figure and Cauchy's theorem. (We use the principal branch of \(z^p\); the small
arc of radius \(\varepsilon\) keeps the path away from the branch point \(0\).)
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.30\textwidth}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.75,>=stealth]
  \draw[->,gray] (-0.2,0)--(3.2,0) node[above]{\scriptsize$\operatorname{Re}z$};
  \draw[->,gray] (0,-0.2)--(0,1.9);
  \draw[blue!70!black,thick,decoration={markings,
      mark=at position 0.2 with {\arrow{>}},
      mark=at position 0.47 with {\arrow{>}},
      mark=at position 0.75 with {\arrow{>}}},postaction={decorate}]
    (0.35,0)--(2.7,0) arc[start angle=0,end angle=35,radius=2.7]--(35:0.35)
    arc[start angle=35,end angle=0,radius=0.35];
  \draw[gray] (0.9,0) arc[start angle=0,end angle=35,radius=0.9];
  \node[font=\scriptsize] at (1.45,0.35) {$\tfrac{\pi}{2p}$};
  \node[font=\scriptsize] at (2.7,-0.25) {$R$};
  \node[font=\scriptsize] at (0.35,-0.25) {$\varepsilon$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\textbf{Step 1: the slanted ray.} Parametrize it by \(z=re^{i\pi/(2p)}\), \(r\ge0\). Then
\[
  z^p=r^p\,e^{ip\cdot\pi/(2p)}=r^p\,e^{i\pi/2}=i\,r^p,
  \qquad
  iz^p=i\cdot i\,r^p=-r^p,
  \qquad
  dz=e^{i\pi/(2p)}\,dr .
\]
So on the ray \(e^{iz^p}=e^{-r^p}\): pure decay. Going outward and using Example~1 \eqref{eq:exp-xp},
\[
  \int_{\mathrm{ray}}e^{iz^p}\,dz
   =e^{i\pi/(2p)}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^p}\,dr
   =e^{i\pi/(2p)}\,\Gamma\left(1+\frac1p\right).
\]
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 2: the outer arc vanishes as \(R\to\infty\).} On it \(z=Re^{i\theta}\),
\(0\le\theta\le\pi/(2p)\), and \(|dz|=R\,d\theta\). The size of the integrand is
\(|e^{iz^p}|=e^{\operatorname{Re}(iz^p)}\), and
\[
 iz^p=iR^pe^{ip\theta}=iR^p\bigl(\cos p\theta+i\sin p\theta\bigr)
 =-R^p\sin p\theta+iR^p\cos p\theta,
\]
so \(|e^{iz^p}|=e^{-R^p\sin p\theta}\). Bound the integral by (size of integrand)\(\times|dz|\):
\[
 \left|\int_{\text{outer arc}}e^{iz^p}dz\right|
 \le\int_0^{\pi/(2p)}e^{-R^p\sin(p\theta)}\,R\,d\theta .
\]
Substitute \(\varphi=p\theta\) (\(d\theta=d\varphi/p\); \(\theta=\pi/(2p)\) becomes \(\varphi=\pi/2\)):
\[
 \int_0^{\pi/(2p)}e^{-R^p\sin(p\theta)}\,R\,d\theta
 =\frac Rp\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{-R^p\sin\varphi}\,d\varphi .
\]
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 2, continued.} On \([0,\pi/2]\) the graph of \(\sin\varphi\) lies above its chord from
\((0,0)\) to \((\pi/2,1)\), so \(\sin\varphi\ge2\varphi/\pi\). A larger exponent of \(e^{-(\cdot)}\) gives a smaller value, so
\[
 \frac Rp\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{-R^p\sin\varphi}\,d\varphi
 \le\frac Rp\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{-2R^p\varphi/\pi}\,d\varphi
 =\frac Rp\left[-\frac{\pi}{2R^p}e^{-2R^p\varphi/\pi}\right]_0^{\pi/2}
\]
\[
 =\frac Rp\cdot\frac{\pi}{2R^p}\bigl(1-e^{-R^p}\bigr)
 =\frac{\pi}{2p}\,R^{1-p}\bigl(1-e^{-R^p}\bigr).
\]
Since \(p>1\), \(R^{1-p}\to0\), so the outer arc vanishes. (This is where \(p>1\) is needed. For
\(p=1\), \(\int_0^\infty\cos x\,dx\) does not converge.)

\textbf{Step 3: the inner arc vanishes as \(\varepsilon\to0\).} There \(|e^{iz^p}|\le1\) (by the
computation of Step~2 with \(\varepsilon\) for \(R\)), and the arc has length \(\varepsilon\pi/(2p)\to0\).
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 4: Cauchy's theorem.} The integrand has no singularity inside the sector, so the
integral around its boundary is zero. Going around: out along the real axis, along the outer
arc, back in along the ray (opposite to the outward direction of Step~1, hence a minus sign),
and along the inner arc. Let \(R\to\infty\), \(\varepsilon\to0\); the two arcs drop out:
\[
  \int_0^\infty e^{ix^p}\,dx-\int_{\mathrm{ray}}e^{iz^p}\,dz=0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \int_0^\infty e^{ix^p}\,dx
   =e^{i\pi/(2p)}\,\Gamma\left(1+\frac1p\right).
\]
\textbf{Step 5: take the real part.} \(e^{i\pi/(2p)}=\cos\frac{\pi}{2p}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{2p}\), and
\(\operatorname{Re}e^{ix^p}=\cos(x^p)\). So
\begin{equation}
  \int_0^\infty\cos(x^p)\,dx
   =\Gamma\left(1+\frac1p\right)
    \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2p}\right),
  \qquad p>1.
  \label{eq:cos-xp}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Check} at \(p=2\) (the Fresnel integral):
\(\int_0^\infty\cos(x^2)\,dx=\Gamma(\tfrac32)\cos\tfrac\pi4=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}=\sqrt{\pi/8}\approx0.627\).
(Taking the imaginary part in Step~5 instead gives \(\int_0^\infty\sin(x^p)\,dx=\Gamma(1+\frac1p)\sin\frac{\pi}{2p}\).)
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Stirling's approximation}
\footnotesize

\textbf{Why we want it.} Factorials grow very fast: \(10!=3\,628\,800\) and
\(20!\approx2.4\times10^{18}\). Counting problems need \(n!\) for huge \(n\), where computing the product directly is impractical. We
want a simple formula that is accurate when \(n\) is large.

\textbf{Goal.} Show that, as \(n\to\infty\),
\[
  n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\,\Bigl(\frac ne\Bigr)^n,
\]
where \(A\sim B\) means that the ratio \(A/B\) tends to \(1\).

\textbf{Starting point.} By \(\Gamma(n+1)=n!\) and Euler's integral \eqref{eq:gamma-def} with
\(z=n+1\),
\[
  n!=\int_0^\infty t^n e^{-t}\,dt .
\]
\textbf{Plan (Laplace's method, Chapter~1).} For large \(n\) the integrand has one tall, narrow
peak. Near the peak it looks like a Gaussian bell curve. We replace the integrand by that Gaussian,
whose integral we know.
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 1: find the peak of \(t^ne^{-t}\).} It is easier to maximize the logarithm,
\(\log(t^ne^{-t})=n\log t-t\). Its derivative is
\[
  \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(n\log t-t\bigr)=\frac nt-1,
\]
which is zero at \(t=n\). The second derivative \(-n/t^2\) is negative, so this is a maximum. The
peak value is \(n^ne^{-n}\).

\textbf{Step 2: move the peak to \(x=1\).} Substitute \(t=nx\), so \(dt=n\,dx\), and \(t=0,\infty\)
becomes \(x=0,\infty\):
\[
  n!=\int_0^\infty (nx)^n e^{-nx}\,n\,dx
   =n^{n+1}\int_0^\infty x^n e^{-nx}\,dx .
\]
Write \(x^n=e^{n\log x}\), so \(x^ne^{-nx}=e^{n(\log x-x)}\). At the peak \(x=1\) the exponent is
\(n(0-1)=-n\). Pull that constant out by adding and subtracting \(1\):
\[
  n!=n^{n+1}e^{-n}\int_0^\infty e^{n\,h(x)}\,dx,
  \qquad
  h(x)=\log x-x+1 .
\]
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 3: the shape of \(h\) near its maximum.} Compute the derivatives:
\[
  h(x)=\log x-x+1,\qquad h'(x)=\frac1x-1,\qquad h''(x)=-\frac1{x^2},\qquad h'''(x)=\frac2{x^3}.
\]
At \(x=1\): \(h(1)=0\), \(h'(1)=0\), \(h''(1)=-1\), \(h'''(1)=2\). Since \(h''<0\) everywhere, \(h\)
has a single maximum, at \(x=1\), with value \(0\). The Taylor series about \(x=1\) is therefore
\[
  h(x)=0+0\cdot(x-1)-\frac12(x-1)^2+\frac{2}{3!}(x-1)^3+\cdots
      =-\frac12(x-1)^2+\frac13(x-1)^3+\cdots .
\]
\textbf{Step 4: the width of the peak.} Keeping only the quadratic term,
\[
  e^{n\,h(x)}\approx e^{-n(x-1)^2/2}.
\]
This is close to \(1\) when \(n(x-1)^2\) is small and tiny when \(n(x-1)^2\) is large. So the peak
has width about \(1/\sqrt n\): it gets narrower as \(n\) grows.
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 5: zoom in on the peak.} Measure the distance from \(x=1\) in units of the width:
\[
  x=1+\frac{u}{\sqrt n},\qquad dx=\frac{du}{\sqrt n}.
\]
As \(x\) runs from \(0\) to \(\infty\), \(u\) runs from \(-\sqrt n\) to \(\infty\). Insert
\(x-1=u/\sqrt n\) into the Taylor series of Step~3 and multiply by \(n\):
\[
  n\,h(x)=n\Bigl[-\frac12\frac{u^2}{n}+\frac13\frac{u^3}{n^{3/2}}+\cdots\Bigr]
  =-\frac{u^2}{2}+\frac{u^3}{3\sqrt n}+\cdots .
\]
As \(n\to\infty\), every term after the first tends to \(0\). So
\[
  \int_0^\infty e^{n\,h(x)}\,dx
   =\frac1{\sqrt n}\int_{-\sqrt n}^{\infty}e^{n\,h}\,du
   \;\approx\;\frac1{\sqrt n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-u^2/2}\,du .
\]
In the last step we also replaced the lower limit \(-\sqrt n\) by \(-\infty\): the integrand is
extremely small there, so this changes almost nothing. (Both approximations become exact in the
limit \(n\to\infty\); we accept this without the detailed estimates.)
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 6: the Gaussian integral.} Substitute \(u=\sqrt2\,v\), \(du=\sqrt2\,dv\), and use
\(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-v^2}dv=\sqrt\pi\) \eqref{eq:gauss-int}:
\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-u^2/2}\,du=\sqrt2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-v^2}\,dv=\sqrt{2\pi}.
\]
Hence
\[
  \int_0^\infty e^{n\,h(x)}\,dx\;\sim\;\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sqrt n}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{n}} .
\]
\textbf{Step 7: put everything together.} Insert this into the result of Step~2, and use
\(n^{n+1}/\sqrt n=n^n\sqrt n\):
\begin{equation}
 n!\sim n^{n+1}e^{-n}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{n}}
   =n^ne^{-n}\sqrt{2\pi n}
   =\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac ne\right)^n.
 \label{eq:stirling}
\end{equation}
This is Chapter~1's Laplace formula with \(s=n\), \(\varphi=h\), \(g=1\), \(x_0=1\), \(|h''(1)|=1\).

\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{How good is it?} At \(n=10\):
\[
  10!=3\,628\,800,\qquad \sqrt{20\pi}\,\Bigl(\frac{10}{e}\Bigr)^{10}\approx3\,598\,696 .
\]
The formula is only \(0.8\%\) low, and the relative error keeps shrinking as \(n\) grows.

\textbf{Logarithmic form.} Taking \(\log\) of \eqref{eq:stirling} (the ratio tends to \(1\), so the
difference of the logarithms tends to \(0\)):
\[
  \log(n!)=n\log n-n+\tfrac12\log(2\pi n)+o(1),
\]
where \(o(1)\) means a quantity that tends to \(0\) as \(n\to\infty\). For very large \(n\), the first
two terms \(n\log n-n\) already dominate; this crude form is common in statistical physics.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Summary of gamma formulas}
\footnotesize
\begin{align*}
  \Gamma(z)
   &=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}t^{z-1}\,dt,
     &&\operatorname{Re}z>0,\\[2pt]
  \Gamma(z+1)
   &=z\Gamma(z),
     &&\Gamma(n+1)=n!\ \ (n\in\mathbb N_0),\\[2pt]
  \Gamma\left(\tfrac12\right)
   &=\sqrt\pi,
     &&\Gamma\left(m+\tfrac12\right)=\frac{(2m)!}{4^m m!}\sqrt\pi,\\[2pt]
  \Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)
   &=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi z)},
     &&z\notin\mathbb Z,\\[2pt]
  n!
   &\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac ne\right)^n,
     &&n\to\infty.
\end{align*}
The continuation \eqref{eq:gamma-continue} has simple poles at \(0,-1,-2,\dots\), with residue
\((-1)^n/n!\) at \(z=-n\), and \(\Gamma\) has no zeros.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Definition and trigonometric form}
\footnotesize

\textbf{Why we want it.} Integrals of the form \(\int_0^1 t^m(1-t)^n\,dt\) appear often. In
probability, \(t^m(1-t)^n\) is the chance of a particular sequence with \(m\) successes and \(n\)
failures when each success has probability \(t\). In Chapter~7 they appear as integrals of trial
functions. We want a closed formula for them.

\textbf{Definition.} The \emph{Euler beta function} is
\begin{equation}
  B(p,q)
   =\int_0^1 t^{p-1}(1-t)^{q-1}\,dt,
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Re}p>0,\quad \operatorname{Re}q>0.
  \label{eq:beta-def}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Why the conditions?} The integral can fail only at the ends.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Near \(t=0\): the integrand behaves like \(t^{p-1}\), integrable when
        \(\operatorname{Re}p-1>-1\), that is, \(\operatorname{Re}p>0\).
  \item Near \(t=1\): it behaves like \((1-t)^{q-1}\), integrable when \(\operatorname{Re}q>0\).
\end{itemize}
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Property 1: symmetry \(B(p,q)=B(q,p)\).} Substitute \(u=1-t\). Then \(t=1-u\),
\(dt=-du\), and the limits \(t=0,1\) become \(u=1,0\):
\[
 B(p,q)=\int_1^0(1-u)^{p-1}u^{q-1}\,(-du)=\int_0^1u^{q-1}(1-u)^{p-1}\,du=B(q,p).
\]
(The minus sign from \(dt=-du\) was used to swap the limits back to \(0\) and \(1\).)

\textbf{Property 2: a trigonometric form.} The next frame needs \(B\) written with sines and
cosines. Since \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\), the substitution \(t=\sin^2\theta\) makes
\(1-t=\cos^2\theta\). The pieces:
\[
 dt=2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta,\qquad
 t^{p-1}=\sin^{2p-2}\theta,\qquad
 (1-t)^{q-1}=\cos^{2q-2}\theta,
\]
and \(t:0\to1\) becomes \(\theta:0\to\pi/2\). Substituting and collecting powers
(\(2p-2+1=2p-1\), \(2q-2+1=2q-1\)):
\begin{equation}
  B(p,q)
   =\int_0^{\pi/2}
      \sin^{2p-2}\theta\,
      \cos^{2q-2}\theta\,
      2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta
   =2\int_0^{\pi/2}
      \sin^{2p-1}\theta
      \cos^{2q-1}\theta\,d\theta.
  \label{eq:beta-trig}
\end{equation}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Beta in terms of gamma}
\footnotesize

\begin{block}{Beta--gamma identity}
For \(\operatorname{Re}p>0\) and \(\operatorname{Re}q>0\),
\[
  B(p,q)=\frac{\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)}{\Gamma(p+q)}.
\]
\end{block}
\textbf{Why it matters.} Once we know \(\Gamma\), we know \(B\). Every beta integral becomes a
ratio of gamma values.

\textbf{Strategy.} The same trick as for the Gaussian integral: multiply two gamma integrals,
get a double integral over a quadrant, and switch to polar coordinates. The radial integral will
give \(\Gamma(p+q)\), and the angular integral will give \(B(p,q)\).

\textbf{Step 1: two gamma integrals in Gaussian form.} By \eqref{eq:gamma-gauss}, with
different variable names,
\[
  \Gamma(p)=2\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}x^{2p-1}\,dx,
  \qquad
  \Gamma(q)=2\int_0^\infty e^{-y^2}y^{2q-1}\,dy.
\]
Multiply them. The product of two single integrals is a double integral over the first quadrant
\(x,y\ge0\), and \(e^{-x^2}e^{-y^2}=e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\):
\[
  \Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)
   =4\int_0^\infty\!\!\int_0^\infty
     e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,
     x^{2p-1}y^{2q-1}\,dx\,dy.
\]
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 2: polar coordinates.} Set \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\). The first quadrant
is \(r\ge0\), \(0\le\theta\le\pi/2\), and \(dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta\). Each piece of the integrand:
\[
 e^{-(x^2+y^2)}=e^{-r^2},\qquad
 x^{2p-1}=r^{2p-1}\cos^{2p-1}\theta,\qquad
 y^{2q-1}=r^{2q-1}\sin^{2q-1}\theta .
\]
Collect the powers of \(r\), including the \(r\) from \(dx\,dy\):
\(r^{2p-1}\cdot r^{2q-1}\cdot r=r^{2p+2q-1}=r^{2(p+q)-1}\). The integrand is now (function of
\(r\))\(\times\)(function of \(\theta\)), so the double integral splits into a product:
\[
 \Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)
 =4\left(\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r^{2(p+q)-1}\,dr\right)
   \left(\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^{2p-1}\theta\sin^{2q-1}\theta\,d\theta\right).
\]
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 3: recognize the two factors.} Write \(4=2\cdot2\) and give one \(2\) to each factor.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Radial factor: this is \eqref{eq:gamma-gauss} with \(z=p+q\):
        \[
          2\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r^{2(p+q)-1}\,dr=\Gamma(p+q).
        \]
  \item Angular factor: this is the trigonometric form \eqref{eq:beta-trig} with the roles of
        \(p\) and \(q\) swapped (\(\sin\) carries \(q\), \(\cos\) carries \(p\)), so it equals \(B(q,p)\),
        which is \(B(p,q)\) by symmetry:
        \[
          2\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2q-1}\theta\cos^{2p-1}\theta\,d\theta=B(q,p)=B(p,q).
        \]
\end{itemize}
So \(\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)=\Gamma(p+q)\,B(p,q)\).

\textbf{Step 4: divide.} \(\Gamma(p+q)\neq0\), because \(\Gamma\) is never zero. Dividing by it,
\begin{equation}
  B(p,q)
   =\frac{\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)}{\Gamma(p+q)}.
  \label{eq:beta-gamma}
\end{equation}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Integer values and how to use beta}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Integer arguments.} Let \(m,n=0,1,2,\dots\). Put \(p=m+1\), \(q=n+1\) in
\eqref{eq:beta-gamma} and use \(\Gamma(k+1)=k!\):
\[
 B(m+1,n+1)=\frac{\Gamma(m+1)\,\Gamma(n+1)}{\Gamma(m+n+2)}=\frac{m!\,n!}{(m+n+1)!}.
\]
To see the binomial coefficient, write \((m+n+1)!=(m+n+1)\cdot(m+n)!\) and recall
\(\binom{m+n}{m}=\frac{(m+n)!}{m!\,n!}\):
\begin{equation}
 B(m+1,n+1)=\frac{m!\,n!}{(m+n+1)\,(m+n)!}
 =\frac1{(m+n+1)\binom{m+n}{m}}.
 \label{eq:beta-integer}
\end{equation}
So beta is a continuous version of \(1/\)(binomial coefficient).

\textbf{A probability reading.} Pick a coin whose heads-probability \(t\) is uniformly random in
\([0,1]\), then toss it \(m+n\) times. Given \(t\), the chance of exactly \(m\) heads is
\(\binom{m+n}{m}t^m(1-t)^n\). Averaging over \(t\) means integrating over \([0,1]\); by
\eqref{eq:beta-integer},
\[
 \int_0^1\binom{m+n}{m}t^m(1-t)^n\,dt=\binom{m+n}{m}B(m+1,n+1)=\frac1{m+n+1}.
\]
The answer does not depend on \(m\): all \(m+n+1\) possible head counts \(0,1,\dots,m+n\) are equally
likely.
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{How to use beta, in practice.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Bring the integral to one of the two standard shapes:
        \(t^{p-1}(1-t)^{q-1}\) on \([0,1]\) \eqref{eq:beta-def}, or
        \(\sin^{2p-1}\theta\cos^{2q-1}\theta\) on \([0,\pi/2]\) \eqref{eq:beta-trig}.
  \item Read off \(p\) and \(q\) by matching exponents.
  \item Apply \(B(p,q)=\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)/\Gamma(p+q)\).
\end{enumerate}
The next examples follow exactly these three steps.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{Worked examples: beta integrals}
\footnotesize

\textbf{Example 1.} Evaluate
\[
  I=\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^4}}.
\]
\textbf{Step 1: substitute to get the shape \(u^{p-1}(1-u)^{q-1}\).} The troublesome piece is
\(1-x^4\), so set \(u=x^4\). Then
\[
  x=u^{1/4},
  \qquad
  dx=\frac14u^{-3/4}\,du,
  \qquad
  \frac1{\sqrt{1-x^4}}=(1-u)^{-1/2},
\]
and the limits \(x=0,1\) become \(u=0,1\). So
\[
  I=\int_0^1(1-u)^{-1/2}\,\frac14u^{-3/4}\,du=\frac14\int_0^1u^{-3/4}(1-u)^{-1/2}\,du .
\]
\textbf{Step 2: read off \(p\) and \(q\).} Compare with \(u^{p-1}(1-u)^{q-1}\):
\[
  p-1=-\tfrac34\ \Rightarrow\ p=\tfrac14,\qquad
  q-1=-\tfrac12\ \Rightarrow\ q=\tfrac12,\qquad
  p+q=\tfrac34 .
\]
\textbf{Step 3: apply the identity}, with \(\Gamma(\frac12)=\sqrt\pi\):
\begin{equation}
  I=\frac14\,B\Bigl(\frac14,\frac12\Bigr)
   =\frac14\,\frac{\Gamma(1/4)\,\Gamma(1/2)}{\Gamma(3/4)}
   =\frac{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(1/4)}{4\,\Gamma(3/4)}\approx1.3110 .
  \label{eq:beta-ex1}
\end{equation}
(Numerically \(\Gamma(\frac14)\approx3.6256\) and \(\Gamma(\frac34)\approx1.2254\).)
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Example 2.} Evaluate
\[
  I_n=\int_0^\pi\sin^n x\,dx,
  \qquad n>-1 .
\]
(The condition \(n>-1\) makes the integral converge near \(x=0\) and \(x=\pi\), where \(\sin x\to0\).)

\textbf{Step 1: reduce to \([0,\pi/2]\).} Split \(\int_0^\pi=\int_0^{\pi/2}+\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi}\). In the
second piece substitute \(x=\pi-y\): then \(dx=-dy\), the limits \(x=\pi/2,\pi\) become
\(y=\pi/2,0\), and \(\sin(\pi-y)=\sin y\). So
\[
  \int_{\pi/2}^{\pi}\sin^nx\,dx=\int_{\pi/2}^{0}\sin^ny\,(-dy)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^ny\,dy .
\]
The two halves are equal, hence
\[
  I_n=2\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^n x\,dx .
\]
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 2: read off \(p\) and \(q\).} Compare with the trigonometric form
\eqref{eq:beta-trig}, \(B(p,q)=2\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2p-1}x\cos^{2q-1}x\,dx\). There is no cosine, so
its exponent is \(0\):
\[
 2p-1=n,\quad 2q-1=0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p=\frac{n+1}{2},\quad q=\frac12,\quad p+q=\frac{n+2}{2}.
\]
\textbf{Step 3: apply the identity}, with \(\Gamma(\frac12)=\sqrt\pi\):
\begin{equation}
 I_n=B\left(\frac{n+1}{2},\frac12\right)
 =\frac{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{n+1}2\bigr)\,\Gamma\bigl(\frac12\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{n+2}2\bigr)}
 =\frac{\sqrt\pi\;\Gamma\bigl(\frac{n+1}2\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{n+2}2\bigr)}.
 \label{eq:beta-ex2}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Check} at \(n=1\): \(\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(1)/\Gamma(\frac32)=\sqrt\pi/(\sqrt\pi/2)=2\), and indeed
\(\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx=[-\cos x]_0^\pi=2\).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks=1.00]{From the \(p\)-series to \(\zeta(s)\)}
\footnotesize

\textbf{Why.} Sums \(\sum n^{-s}\) appear in physics: black-body radiation (end of this frame) and
the string spectrum of Chapter~7. We want to write such a sum in the same integral language as
\(\Gamma\).

\textbf{Goal.} Show that the series \(\sum_n n^{-s}\) equals a single integral:
\[
 \zeta(s)=\frac1{\Gamma(s)}\int_0^\infty\frac{t^{s-1}}{e^t-1}\,dt
 \qquad(\text{this is \eqref{eq:zeta-int} below}).
\]

\textbf{Step 1: define \(\zeta\).} For complex \(s\) with \(\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s>1\), define
\begin{equation}
  \zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^s},
  \qquad\text{where } n^{-s}=e^{-s\log n}.
  \label{eq:zeta-series}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Why \(\operatorname{Re}s>1\)?} Write \(s=\sigma+i\tau\). Then
\(|n^{-s}|=|e^{-\sigma\log n}|\,|e^{-i\tau\log n}|=e^{-\sigma\log n}\cdot1=n^{-\sigma}\). The real series
\(\sum n^{-\sigma}\) converges exactly when \(\sigma>1\) (integral test:
\(\int_1^\infty x^{-\sigma}dx\) is finite exactly when \(\sigma>1\)). At \(s=1\) we get the harmonic
series, which diverges since \(H_n>\log(n+1)\).
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 2: each term is a gamma integral.} Consider \(\int_0^\infty t^{s-1}e^{-nt}\,dt\) and
substitute \(u=nt\), to turn \(e^{-nt}\) into \(e^{-u}\). Then \(t=u/n\), \(dt=du/n\), and
\(t^{s-1}=u^{s-1}n^{-(s-1)}\). The powers of \(n\) combine: \(n^{-(s-1)}\cdot n^{-1}=n^{-s}\). So
\begin{equation}
  \int_0^\infty t^{s-1}e^{-nt}\,dt
   =\int_0^\infty
     \left(\frac{u}{n}\right)^{s-1}
     e^{-u}\,\frac{du}{n}
   =n^{-s}\int_0^\infty u^{s-1}e^{-u}\,du
   =n^{-s}\,\Gamma(s).
  \label{eq:zeta-term}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Step 3: add up the terms.} Divide \eqref{eq:zeta-term} by \(\Gamma(s)\) and sum over \(n\).
Moving the sum inside the integral (allowed here; we accept this step),
\[
  \zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-s}
  =\frac1{\Gamma(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_0^\infty t^{s-1}e^{-nt}\,dt
  =\frac1{\Gamma(s)}\int_0^\infty t^{s-1}\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-nt}\Bigr)dt .
\]
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Step 4: sum the geometric series.} For \(t>0\), put \(q=e^{-t}\), so \(0<q<1\). Then
\(\sum_{n\ge1}q^n=q+q^2+\cdots=q/(1-q)\). Multiplying top and bottom by \(e^t\),
\[
  \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-nt}
   =\frac{e^{-t}}{1-e^{-t}}
   =\frac1{e^t-1}.
\]
\textbf{Result.} Insert this into Step~3:
\begin{equation}
  \zeta(s)
   =\frac1{\Gamma(s)}
     \int_0^\infty\frac{t^{s-1}}{e^t-1}\,dt,
  \qquad \operatorname{Re}s>1.
  \label{eq:zeta-int}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Consistency check.} Near \(t=0\), \(e^t-1\approx t\), so the integrand behaves like
\(t^{s-1}/t=t^{s-2}\). That is integrable at \(0\) exactly when \(\operatorname{Re}s-2>-1\), that is,
\(\operatorname{Re}s>1\): the same condition as for the series.
\par\framebreak\par
\textbf{Two reference values} (quoted here without proof):
\[
  \zeta(2)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}\approx1.6449,\qquad
  \zeta(4)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{90}.
\]
Check: the first \(100\) terms of \(\zeta(2)\) already sum to \(1.6350\). The first is the Basel
identity; Chapter~7 uses it for the string spectrum.

\textbf{Application: black-body radiation.} In Planck's law, with \(t=h\nu/(k_BT)\), the factor
\(1/(e^t-1)\) is the average number of photons in a mode. The total radiated energy (the
Stefan--Boltzmann \(T^4\) law) contains the integral \(\int_0^\infty t^3/(e^t-1)\,dt\). By
\eqref{eq:zeta-int} with \(s=4\), together with \(\Gamma(4)=3!=6\),
\[
  \int_0^\infty\frac{t^3}{e^t-1}\,dt=\Gamma(4)\,\zeta(4)=6\cdot\frac{\pi^4}{90}=\frac{\pi^4}{15}.
\]
\end{frame}




\begin{frame}{Where we have been}
\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gamma:}
        \(\Gamma(n+1)=n!\),
        \(\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)\),
        \(\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi\), and
        \(\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\pi/\sin(\pi z)\) for
        \(z\notin\mathbb Z\); poles at \(0,-1,-2,\dots\) with residue
        \((-1)^n/n!\) \eqref{eq:gamma-residue}; Stirling
        \(n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n\) \eqref{eq:stirling}.
  \item \textbf{Beta:}
        \[
          B(p,q)
           =\frac{\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)}{\Gamma(p+q)}
           =\int_0^1t^{p-1}(1-t)^{q-1}\,dt,
          \qquad \operatorname{Re}p,\operatorname{Re}q>0.
        \]
  \item \textbf{Zeta:}
        \[
          \zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-s}
           =\frac1{\Gamma(s)}\int_0^\infty\frac{t^{s-1}}{e^t-1}\,dt,
          \qquad \operatorname{Re}s>1,
        \]
        with the quoted Basel value \(\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6\).
\end{itemize}
Gamma and beta are the core language; later special functions are largely built from them.
\end{frame}
